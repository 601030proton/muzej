The Venus of Skupi

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Discovered in a Roman bathhouse at Scupi in 2008, this marble Venus reveals the public life of an ancient city near Skopje.

In the excavation photograph, Venus lies on her back in the earth. The pale torso is exposed; the surrounding soil still holds the outline of the find. It is an unusually direct encounter with a sculpture more often considered upright, isolated against a museum wall. Here, before reconstruction, the figure belongs to the ground.

Archaeologists uncovered her in 2008 in the large Roman bath complex at Scupi, on the outskirts of present-day Skopje. Known in antiquity as Colonia Flavia Scupinorum, the city had the public buildings and civic ambitions of a Roman colony. The baths were part of that urban life, and the statue was part of their furnishing.

The sculpture is dated to the second century CE. Macedonian archaeological cataloguing identifies its stone as fine-grained white marble from Paros. Both the material and the subject connect the find to a much wider Mediterranean world: a prized sculptural stone and a goddess whose image had already been reworked for centuries.

Yet the place of discovery gives this Venus her particular history. Speaking to Archaeology magazine in 2008, Skopje City Museum archaeologist Marina Ončevska Todorovska described a figure intended to be seen by people relaxing in the water. The statue offers a glimpse of the baths beyond their surviving masonry: a building furnished for looking as well as bathing.

Gesture

Her weight rests on the left leg. The opposite leg relaxes, shifting the hips and setting the torso slightly off its vertical axis. This arrangement, known as contrapposto, makes the standing body appear capable of movement. Across it, the arms establish a second rhythm: one hand rises towards the breast, while the other reaches down across the pelvis.

The pose belongs to the Venus pudica tradition. Pudica is usually translated as “modest” or “chaste,” but the name describes a convention of representation. The hands cover the body while directing the viewer’s attention towards it. The figure’s apparent withdrawal is also the means by which the sculptor presents her.

The tradition reaches back to the Aphrodite of Knidos, made by Praxiteles in the fourth century BCE. That lost sculpture established an influential image of the nude goddess at her bath. Later sculptors varied the position of the hands, the turn of the head and the balance of the body. The Scupi Venus belongs to this later Roman tradition, in which an established pose could be adapted to a different setting.

Look closely at the surviving head. Waves of hair are drawn back from the forehead and gathered into a knot above it. The incised strands give the hair a density that contrasts with the broader, smoother planes of the cheeks. From the front, the face appears composed; in profile, the projecting knot and gathered hair give the head a more intricate silhouette. The photographs reward looking from more than one side.

Among the bathers

A dolphin rises beside the left leg, its tail pointing upwards. It is an attribute of Venus, recalling the goddess’s association with the sea. It also answers a practical problem. A large marble figure places considerable weight on narrow ankles; an adjoining form can help brace the stone. In this sculptural tradition, the supporting element carries meaning as well as weight.

In the baths, the marine creature would have belonged to a setting already organized around water and the body. The connection is particularly apt: a nude goddess associated with bathing placed within a building where visitors undressed, washed and spent time together. The sculpture’s subject and its architectural setting reinforce one another.

Venus was not the only divine figure represented there. In her study of stone objects from the thermal complex, Ončevska Todorovska records fragments of Hygieia, the goddess associated with health, and an unidentified male deity, alongside other carved and inscribed monuments. These finds suggest a richly furnished interior rather than a solitary masterpiece set apart from its surroundings.

The distinction changes how we look at the statue. In the museum, attention settles on a single object. At Scupi, it belonged to an ensemble of architecture, sculpture and water. Its scale, pose and material contributed to the character of a public room. To understand the Venus is also to imagine the space that once accommodated her, without pretending that its full arrangement can now be recovered.

Reassembled

The standing figure is also the result of modern work. The statue was recovered in sixteen fragments, with parts of the lower legs and feet missing. Conservation undertaken in 2010–11 had to restore its balance as well as reconnect the marble.

Jure Balić and Siniša Bizjak’s published account describes the examination of the stone and soluble salts, the cleaning of joins, and the use of stainless-steel dowels. A separate support followed the anatomy of the missing leg and foot. First worked out in wood, it was made in steel and connected to a base; reconstructed sections in artificial marble concealed the reinforcement.

Crucially, the support was designed to be detachable. If the missing ancient fragments are found, the assembly can be adjusted or removed to accommodate them.

The reassembled figure can appear complete at first glance. Beneath its surface, however, the support preserves a distinction between the ancient marble that survives and the parts that may yet be found.
