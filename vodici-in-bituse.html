Ritual

Vodici in Bituše

Every January, the women of a mountain village carry bread and song through a celebration that brings families home.

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

At midnight before Vasilica, three girls make their way to the Bigrišča fountain. Coins are placed in the water, dogwood branches are gathered, and gunshots mark the occasion. The water will be used to prepare ceremonial bread. In Bituše, a mountain village on the slopes of Dešat, Vodici has begun.

Here, the Orthodox feast of Epiphany unfolds over several days. Crosses travel between households and Saint John Bigorski Monastery; women sing for particular families and carry bread through the village. Two kumovi hold the ritual responsibility, which passes to new households each year. In 2019, the celebration received official recognition as intangible cultural heritage of exceptional significance.

Bread and water

Ethnographers Fima Atanasova and Vladimir Bočev documented these preparations during fieldwork in 1990 and 1991. At the homes of the incoming kumovi, a girl whose parents were both living kneaded an odd number of loaves, often five, seven, or nine, into a circular bread. A portion was kept throughout the year beside the ritual cross.

On January 17, two women stood on stools at opposite sides of a table and twisted two red threads and one white while the gathering sang. The outgoing kumovi then took the crosses to Saint John Bigorski Monastery, where the water was blessed. The crosses returned to Bituše with ajazmo, holy water carried to village households.

The kumstarki

On Vodici, the women known as kumstarki gather in festive Mijak dress: embroidered garments, woven aprons, jewelry, and carefully arranged head coverings. They balance wicker trays of ceremonial bread on their heads, cushioned by white kerchiefs, and set out toward the church of Saint Archangel Michael.

They sing as they go. The verses address Saint John and the families entrusted with the crosses; names can enter the songs, making each performance particular to the household. Before reaching the churchyard, the women sing “Прелетал ми сиви сокол,” one of the songs recorded in the 1990–91 fieldwork.

Some participants live elsewhere for much of the year. The January gathering brings them back to Bituše, where the procession still moves through the same village streets.

The songs

“Добре ми дојде, Свети Јоване” — “Welcome, Saint John” — accompanies several moments in the celebration. The songs are tied to actions: preparing bread, welcoming a kum, joining the procession, or saying farewell.

In a 2025 study, ethnomusicologist Melita Ivanovska documented four songs performed in the contemporary celebration: “Добре ми дојде, Свети Јоване,” “Јадите, пијте, веселите се,” “Петор и Павле две ора водет,” and “Збогум, со здравје, Свети Јоване.” Earlier accounts preserve additional songs. Words and verses have changed, while familiar local melodies and rhythms remain recognizable.

Jovanovo oro

At the church, the women gather for Sveti Jovanovo oro. Atanasova and Bočev observed three to five rounds of the ceremonial dance. Sometimes the singing stopped before the steps were finished. The women continued in silence, completing what the researchers called nemo oro.

The bread is later divided and shared. In the 1990–91 account, it was distributed at the village school for health. The crosses pass to new households the following year, and with them the work of preparing the ceremony.

Next January, families will return to Bituše. There will be bread to prepare, songs to remember, and another procession through the village.
