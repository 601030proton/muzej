Ritual

Vodici in Bituše

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Photographs Maja Janevska-Ilieva / Nova Makedonija

Every January, the women of a mountain village carry bread and song through a celebration that brings families home.

In Bituše, women carry homemade bread through the village in wicker baskets balanced on their heads. They sing on the way to the church of Saint Archangel Michael, where they gather for a ceremonial dance. Maja Janevska-Ilieva’s photographs of the January 2024 celebration show children among them, steadying their own baskets beneath the snowy mountains.

This is the village’s celebration of Vodici, the Orthodox feast of Epiphany on January 19, commemorating Christ’s baptism in the Jordan. Here in western Macedonia, the blessing of water is part of a longer sequence of household gatherings, breadmaking, songs, and the transfer of responsibility for two ritual crosses. People whose families come from Bituše return to take part.

Taking the kumstvo

Two men, each representing a household, serve as kumovi. Their responsibility, the kumstvo, involves hosting gatherings, taking the crosses to Saint John Bigorski Monastery, and caring for them through the year. Relatives and friends help carry out the celebration. The women and girls who carry the bread are known as kumstarki.

The promise is made almost a year ahead. On January 31, Tanasovden, the feast of Saint Athanasius, members of the incoming households publicly commit to taking the crosses the following January. Mavrovo National Park’s account describes the pledge outside the village church; ethnographers Fima Anastasovska and Vladimir Bočev recorded it during fieldwork in 1990 and 1991.

Accepting the kumstvo carries religious weight. Anastasovska and Bočev recorded a belief that withdrawing from the promise could bring illness or death as punishment from Saint John. In her 2025 study, ethnomusicologist Melita Ivanovska also describes the responsibility as both an honor associated with the saint’s blessing and an obligation that people fear refusing. The pledge binds the household to the work ahead.

Bread and water

The January preparations begin around Vasilica, the feast of Saint Basil on January 14. Water is collected for the first ceremonial bread. Mavrovo National Park places the gathering at dawn; the 1990–91 fieldwork describes a midnight visit to the Bigrišča fountain on the eve of the feast.

In that older account, three girls from each sponsoring household went to fetch water. Coins were thrown beneath the fountain’s spout, gunshots sounded, and accompanying young people broke dogwood branches to decorate a water jug. The researchers connected the gunfire with driving away harmful spirits and the dogwood with wishes for health.

A girl whose parents were both living kneaded the bread, a qualification specified in the same account. She joined five, seven, or nine loaves into one circular bread. A portion was kept with the cross and dogwood until the following year. More loaves were prepared for the kumstarki to carry.

On January 17, the outgoing households host a farewell gathering before the crosses leave for Bigorski. Anastasovska and Bočev describe two women standing on stools at opposite corners of the room, twisting two red threads and one white into a cord above the table. Guests tossed fruit, chestnuts, and sweets over the table and the kumovi. The researchers interpreted these actions as wishes for abundance and protection.

Women accompany the departing men as far as Lajkovec, singing a farewell to Saint John, before turning back. At the monastery, the crosses are tied together and the water is blessed. The men return with ajazmo, holy water, which is shared with the villagers. On January 18, the kumovi visit homes with the crosses, blessing the households and leaving water. The crosses spend that night in the village church.

January 19

On the morning of Vodici, villagers gather for a church service before walking to the Bituška River for the blessing of water and the retrieval of a cross. Afterward, the kumstarki go to the incoming households to collect their baskets of bread. They form a procession back to the church, where they perform Sveti Jovanovo oro, the ceremonial circle dance named for Saint John.

The baskets have their own order. In the 1990–91 account, the household’s basket came first, followed by those of its relatives and friends. The women carried them on white headcloths and took care not to let them fall, which was considered unlucky.

Anastasovska and Bočev describe three to five dances in the churchyard. If the singing finished before the dance did, the women continued the steps without it. The researchers called this nemo oro, the silent dance.

The songs change

The repertoire has narrowed. Comparing archival recordings with her own fieldwork, Ivanovska identifies four songs still performed in the tradition. Others, including “Прелетал ми сиви сокол,” once sung approaching the churchyard, were absent from her recent research, including 2023–24. “Добре ми дојде, Свети Јоване” — “Welcome, Saint John” — now recurs at several moments, taking the place of older songs.

Words have changed within the surviving songs, too. In a version of “Јадите, пите, веселите се” recorded in 1968, singers called for beans and brine using terms that, Ivanovska reports, younger generations no longer understood. Those lines were dropped. The newer version combines words from several songs while retaining the earlier musical pattern.

Women remain the principal singers. Ivanovska describes the leading voices at the front of the procession, where younger participants can hear and learn from them. For a family returning to Bituše, joining the line means listening to the people who know when to begin and what to sing.

After the dance, the bread is shared. The 1990–91 account places its distribution at the village school. Nova Makedonija’s January 2024 report describes the gathering in the cultural hall, where the kumstarki eat before the men. The baskets come down, the men cut the loaves, and the women who carried them sit down to eat.
