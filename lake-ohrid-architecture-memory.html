Architecture | Ohrid

The Ohrid House and the Logic of Place

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Why Ohrid’s old houses widen as they rise, and how families lived in them through the seasons.

On a narrow street in Ohrid’s old town, the stone ground floor follows the line of the lot. Look up and the house widens. Timber-framed rooms project over the street, and rows of windows light the upper floors. From the lane you see a wall. From upstairs, you might see across the rooftops to the lake.

The houses that give the old town this silhouette are largely from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, late in a much longer history. Ancient Lychnidos and the town’s Byzantine churches belong to earlier layers of the same hillside. By the late Ottoman period, builders were working within an established network of steep lanes and irregular plots. They adapted a form of town house common across the Ottoman Balkans to the room they had in Ohrid.

Winter below, summer above

The way in could take you past a small yard, where a cellar or summer kitchen might sit. The winter rooms, sometimes on a mezzanine, were smaller, more enclosed, and easier to heat. In warmer months, the family could move upstairs, where the rooms had more light and air. A house could rise three or four floors. Its shape makes more sense when you imagine a family using different parts of it over the course of a year.

At the top of the stairs was the čardak, the hall from which the upper rooms opened. Depending on the house, it might be partly enclosed or open toward a terrace. It could also be a place to sit or sleep in summer. In Serena Acciai’s account of Boris Čipan and Dušan Grabrijan’s work, the orientation of the čardak helps explain how these houses fit together on the slope. Each belongs to a street, but its upper rooms can look over the neighboring roofs.

Velika Ivkovska’s measured plans show how the stairs, hall, and rooms fit together. The L-shaped arrangement she describes is common in Ohrid, with the position of the hall and porch varying between houses and floors. A house that looks a little lopsided from the lane can have a perfectly legible plan once you’re upstairs.

Stone and timber

Stone masonry gave the lower levels their weight and enclosure. Above it, builders used bondruk, a lighter timber-framed system finished in plaster. The change in construction made it possible for upper floors to project beyond the masonry below. On a tight lot, that projection could give a room a more regular shape and put its windows where they could catch more light.

The white plaster could go over a wooden lattice. Lime and river sand were among the ingredients, and finely ground material such as tile dust could be added. Radmila Tomovska and Ana Radivojević describe one wall assembly about seven inches (18 centimeters) thick, with an air space between two timber frames. The wall was light enough for the upper floors and had an insulating layer built into it.

Reading the windows

Back in the street, look at how the windows are grouped. A row of openings can give the upper floor a steady rhythm even when the building below follows an uneven lot. In some houses, the groupings give you a clue to where one room ends and the next begins. The eaves run out beyond the windows, shading them and protecting the plaster beneath.

Inside, the carpentry continued at the scale of the room. Cupboards, shelves, and window frames were built into the places where people used them. A fireplace could organize a winter room as decisively as a view organized an upper one.

Builders from Ohrid, Struga, Debar, and the surrounding region carried techniques between towns. The same broad arrangement could serve a different climate. In Lerin, in Aegean Macedonia, houses also used heavier lower walls and lighter upper framing, but their small winter openings make the demands of a colder climate especially clear. In Ohrid, the upper windows and the lake-facing slope give the arrangement its particular character.

What a façade can hide

UNESCO has warned about the effects of development and inappropriate alterations on the historic town. A renovation can preserve a familiar silhouette while losing much of what made the building work. Consider a timber strip applied to a finished wall. It can reproduce the dark line you see from the street. In a bondruk wall, the timber is the frame, with plaster laid over the lattice between its members.

Walk back down the hill and the house changes again. The windows slip out of sight, and the stone base comes back into view. A few steps earlier you could see where someone might have spent a summer evening. Now you’re close enough to touch the wall that kept the street outside.
