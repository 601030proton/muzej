Tokyo on the Vardar

The 1965 competition for central Skopje, the City Gate proposal, and the housing built as the City Wall.

In the model Kenzo Tange’s team submitted for Skopje in 1965, a person arriving by train could walk toward Republic Square without crossing the streets below. Tange called the raised deck “artificial ground.” It tied the City Gate, a district of transport and commerce, to the City Wall, a perimeter of apartment buildings. The proposal drew on a question he had already posed in his Plan for Tokyo 1960: could a city gain room to grow by building a second ground level above its roads?

Skopje was rebuilding after the earthquake of July 26, 1963, which killed 1,070 people and damaged or destroyed much of the city. Relief came from 78 countries, and Yugoslav institutions and the United Nations worked on reconstruction. Four Yugoslav and four foreign teams entered the competition for the city center. The jury gave Tange’s team 60 percent of the first prize and awarded the other 40 percent to Radovan Miščević and Fedor Wenzler of Zagreb. Local planners and architects then worked with both schemes.

For Tokyo Bay, Tange had proposed an axis of roads and services that could take on new districts over time. This distinction between a long-lived structure and the parts that could change was central to Metabolism, a Japanese architectural movement launched around the 1960 World Design Conference. Tange mentored younger Metabolists and pursued the idea at the scale of a city. Skopje’s City Gate model shows the principle in section: trains, roads, ramps, and the public walkway occupy different levels, with room for buildings to connect to them.

Tange called the deck “artificial ground.”

The joint scheme became known as the “ninth project,” after the eight competition entries. The team had to account for projects already under construction and work out where the new housing would meet existing streets and buildings. Wind-tunnel tests, views, and the scale of nearby housing helped reshape the proposed perimeter. The team broke up the continuous blocks, dropped the stretch on the Vardar’s left bank, and omitted the pedestrian deck from the housing development.

The outline of the built City Wall still turns at the angles Tange drew, with gaps between the apartment blocks and green spaces within the perimeter. Architectural historian Mirjana Lozanovska describes proposals for taller towers alongside apartment blocks about 24 meters high, closer to the scale of existing housing. Macedonian architects including Malenkova, Kosevska, Bogačev, Serafimovski, and Simoski worked on those lower blocks. Their decisions shaped where the housing meets the sidewalk and where a route opens through it.

At the railway station, the plan survived in another form. Tange’s archive records a bus and railway center in reinforced and prestressed concrete, only partly realized. The drawings extended the route from the station into the center on an elevated deck. Today that line breaks at the station. To follow Tange’s plan farther into Skopje, look for the City Wall’s angled run of apartment blocks.
