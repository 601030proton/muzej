Tokyo on the Vardar

Kenzo Tange’s plan for central Skopje, and the decisions that changed it before it was built.

Kenzo Tange’s plan for central Skopje had two organizing elements: a gateway built around transport and a wall made of housing. The City Gate would bring regional movement into the center; the City Wall would surround it with apartments. Between them, streets, public buildings, businesses, and pedestrian routes would form a new city center after the earthquake of July 26, 1963. The plan’s subsequent revisions show how much had to change before those ideas could be built.

The earthquake killed 1,070 people and devastated much of Skopje. Relief arrived from 78 countries. Reconstruction brought together Yugoslav institutions, the United Nations, and international teams working on housing, infrastructure, engineering, and planning. Yugoslavia’s nonaligned position helped make that cooperation possible across Cold War divisions. Tange entered a reconstruction effort already much larger than the city-center project for which he became famous.

In 1965, an international competition invited four Yugoslav and four foreign teams to propose a new center. The jury divided the first prize: 60 percent went to Tange’s team and 40 percent to Radovan Miščević and Fedor Wenzler of Zagreb. The outcome required further collaboration. The competition proposal was a starting point for a working plan, with local planners and architects involved in deciding how it would develop.

Tange brought experience of both reconstruction and metropolitan planning. Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park had addressed the civic meaning of rebuilding after destruction. His Plan for Tokyo 1960 proposed organizing metropolitan growth through a large infrastructural framework. In Skopje, these interests converged within an existing city whose residents needed homes, services, and connections restored.

His approach was closely associated with Metabolism, the movement launched around Tokyo’s World Design Conference in 1960. Its architects understood cities as capable of growth and renewal. They distinguished between structures intended to last—transport routes, service cores, major frameworks—and shorter-lived elements that could be altered or replaced. Buildings and districts would change at different speeds, rather than becoming obsolete together.

Tange was a senior influence on the younger Metabolists, several of whom passed through his University of Tokyo laboratory. His urban proposals gave their interest in growth a metropolitan scale. Skopje offered an opportunity to develop related ideas through a commission with public authorities, construction programs, and an existing urban fabric. It also exposed the difficulty of making a large framework respond to particular streets and buildings.

The City Gate concentrated transport, business, and arrival along a linear route toward Republic Square on the Vardar. Its proposed gateway structure would give a visible form to the point where regional traffic entered the center. Movement was organized across different levels, with pedestrian circulation considered alongside the transport system. The name made this complicated arrangement easy to grasp: it was an entrance to the city.

The City Wall gave housing an equally prominent role. Apartment buildings would form an inhabited perimeter around the central district, establishing its boundary while keeping residents close to its public and commercial life. In the model, the Wall reads as a continuous urban figure. On the ground, its success would depend on the dimensions of individual buildings, the spaces between them, and the routes through the housing.

That difference between a clear figure in a model and a workable residential district became central to the project. The Wall’s height and continuity raised questions about views, airflow, and its relationship to surrounding buildings. A composition that looked coherent from above still had to be tested as a place to live.

The postcompetition development plan became known as the “ninth project,” following the eight competition entries. It was prepared by a mixed team representing the awarded schemes and local institutions. Tange’s proposal remained the principal reference, but the working plan had to account for changing requirements and construction already under way. It was revised through that process.

Research on the City Wall documents changes prompted by wind-tunnel testing and criticism of blocked views, air currents, and the scale of the proposed apartment blocks. The segment on the Vardar’s left bank was abandoned. The initial connected arrangement was reworked, and the raised pedestrian deck was eventually omitted from the housing development. These decisions changed the plan’s physical operation as well as its silhouette.

The eventual combination of towers and lower apartment blocks retained the idea of a residential edge. Shops occupied ground level, with green spaces within the segments. The Wall could still organize the center without reproducing the continuous structure of the competition image. But the loss of the deck also meant the loss of a proposed connection: the built housing no longer offered the same elevated pedestrian route.

Architectural historian Mirjana Lozanovska traces a further change in scale. Later proposals paired apartment buildings about 24 meters high, closer to the height of existing housing, with groups of taller blocks. The perimeter remained legible while the individual buildings acquired different proportions. This was a substantial design decision, affecting how the new housing met the city around it.

Macedonian architects developed those buildings beyond Tange’s planning and massing studies. Lozanovska identifies a team including Malenkova, Kosevska, Bogačev, Serafimovski, and Simoski among those responsible for the horizontal configurations. Their work followed the angled geometry of the residential ring while developing its architectural treatment. Authorship here includes the planning concept, the revisions, and the design of the buildings that residents would occupy.

This makes the usual account of a Japanese architect rebuilding Skopje inadequate. Tange’s contribution was decisive, but the City Wall required decisions by several teams. The wider reconstruction included other planning work, notably that of Doxiadis Associates, and buildings by architects with distinct approaches. The university campus pictured here belongs to that broader reconstruction landscape; it should not be treated as an illustration of Tange’s individual authorship.

The relationship with Metabolism also requires a distinction. A framework designed to accommodate change is one thing; a project altered because its proposed scale or circulation proves unsuitable is another. The revisions to Skopje do not automatically demonstrate that Tange’s theory succeeded. They show which parts of his proposal could be retained and which had to be reconsidered. The residential perimeter survived more clearly than its original architectural form and pedestrian system.

The Gate and Wall nevertheless gave the center a powerful organizing idea. Transport would establish a major route of arrival, and housing would give the central district an inhabited edge. Their names connected unfamiliar large structures with familiar ways of understanding a city. The architectural question was whether those large gestures could support the smaller movements of daily life.

The City Wall offers a concrete way to read the result. Its towers and lower blocks preserve a boundary that can be recognized in Tange’s model. At ground level, shops and green spaces belong to the revised housing scheme. The proposed pedestrian deck is absent. Comparing those three conditions—the retained perimeter, the developed buildings, and the missing route—shows where the plan endured and where the built city took a different course.
