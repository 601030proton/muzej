Traditions

Ajvar

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Macedonia’s autumn pepper harvest and the work behind a jar of ajvar.

Ajvar (ајвар) is a spread of roasted peppers eaten in Macedonia and elsewhere in the Balkans. In Macedonia it is commonly made from ripe red peppers in early autumn and kept in jars as part of the zimnica (зимница), the household’s winter provisions. It is often eaten with bread and white cheese. The name derives from Turkish hayvar, meaning fish roe or caviar.

Peppers and preparation

Slow Food Macedonia records a preparation from Strumica made with kami, a red pepper with broad, flat sides and thick flesh. The flat sides cook evenly on a roasting surface, and the flesh is substantial enough to peel and grind. Agricultural collections in Macedonia also document peppers called kurtovska kapija za ajvar and ajvarski.

After roasting, cooks remove the skins and seeds and grind or chop the flesh. They cook the pulp in a broad pot, stirring as it thickens. The Strumica preparation uses peppers, oil, and salt. The anthropologist Miladina Monova recorded households that also used small amounts of eggplant, carrot, or tomato.

Household production

In Prilep, Monova observed families making ajvar in gardens and in courtyards between apartment buildings. They used one stove to roast the peppers and another to cook the ground pulp. Relatives and neighbors shared stoves and divided the work of roasting, peeling, grinding, and stirring. In the households Monova studied, women did most of the preparation, while men sometimes helped stir the large pots.

In Prilep, preparation could begin later than in Skopje. Monova found that some households waited until the tobacco harvest ended in late September, then turned to roasting peppers.

The spread of the red-pepper preserve

Monova cites interwar Yugoslav accounts of ajvar served in restaurants and at formal gatherings. Some early recipes used green peppers. She links the rise of the red-pepper preserve in Macedonian households to the wider availability of peppers grown for processing and of sunflower oil after the Second World War. In Prilep, ajvar was already a staple among town households by the early 1970s. Only one of the twenty-two households in her study grew its own peppers. The others prepared ajvar with peppers bought at market.
