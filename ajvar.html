Traditions

Ajvar

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Macedonia’s autumn pepper harvest and the work behind a jar of ajvar.

In Prilep, one family told the anthropologist Miladina Monova that they began making ajvar after moving to town. They bought red peppers at the market and cooked them at home. By the early 1970s, ajvar was becoming a staple among the town households Monova studied. The family’s account dates a practice that can feel much older.

Ajvar (ајвар) is a spread made from roasted peppers. In Macedonia, the familiar red version is part of the zimnica (зимница), the household’s winter stores. It shares shelf space with pickled vegetables and fruit preserves. A jar might be opened to eat with bread and white cheese.

The pepper harvest

Ajvar is usually made in early autumn, when ripe red peppers are plentiful. Slow Food Macedonia’s 2019 inventory records a Strumica preparation using kami, a local name for red peppers with flat sides and thick flesh. The shape helps the peppers roast evenly, and the thick walls leave more flesh after peeling. Agricultural research also records kurtovska kapija and several peppers known locally as ajvarski, named for their use in ajvar.

The peppers are roasted, peeled, seeded, and ground or chopped. The pulp cooks down in a broad pot as someone keeps it moving with a wooden paddle. Oil and salt are the only additions in the Strumica account. Monova recorded batches that included some eggplant, carrot, or tomato. The ingredients and the time at the stove vary from one household to the next.

A day around the stove

In the households Monova observed, relatives and neighbors sometimes shared two stoves, one for roasting and one for cooking the pulp. Several jobs could be underway at once. While one person worked the fire, someone else could peel the peppers that had cooled. Women did most of the work in the households she studied. Men often helped with the heavy stirring as the pulp thickened.

Outside space mattered. A yard had room for the fire, the work surface, and the large pot. In Skopje, where many people lived in apartments, Monova found households making arrangements with friends or neighbors who could share space and equipment.

From market to pantry

The name comes from the Turkish hayvar, meaning caviar or salted fish roe. Earlier Yugoslav accounts describe ajvar as a pepper dish served in restaurants and at formal meals. Some of those versions used green peppers. Monova traces the spread of the red winter preserve in Macedonia to the decades after the Second World War, when peppers grown in large quantities for processing and sunflower oil became widely available.

Of the twenty-two households Monova surveyed, only one grew peppers. The others depended on the market for the main ingredient. They judged their ajvar by the peppers they could buy and the work they did at home. A jar could go to a relative or a neighbor who had helped make the batch.
