Irina Janakievska

Interview Aleksandar Trajkovski

Irina Janakievska’s The Balkan Kitchen won the 2025 James Beard Book Award in the International category. She talks about family recipes and learning to make pita with her mother and grandmother.

Your work sits between cookbook, memoir, archive, and cultural history. When did you first realise food could carry that much memory?

There wasn't one particular moment. It was something I came to understand gradually, through my own relationship with food, the process of researching The Balkan Kitchen, and the stories people shared with me along the way.

Some of my earliest memories are of making pita with my mother and grandmother. They would let me stretch and fill my own pastry, although I invariably used far too much flour, and my little pita snail would bake alongside theirs. My grandfather would eat it with extraordinary tenderness and declare it the best pita he'd ever tasted, even when it was hard as a stone. Looking back, I think that was one of my first lessons in food: that what we remember about a dish has as much to do with the people who made it, and the love invested in it, as with how it actually tastes.

When I began researching Balkan food, initially through cookbooks published during the Yugoslav period, I realised that these personal memories were part of a much larger story. Recipes could tell us about migration, cultural exchange, changing borders and the rhythms of everyday life. They could preserve knowledge and experiences that rarely appeared in official histories, and reveal connections between people and places that contemporary national narratives sometimes obscure.

As I began sharing these stories, people from across the diaspora started writing to me about their own family recipes, their grandmothers and the dishes they wished they had learned to make before it was too late. Others shared memories of women who had left the Balkans but carried the tastes and traditions of home with them. I realised I wasn't simply documenting recipes. I was helping to preserve fragments of family and cultural history that might otherwise disappear.

That became one of the central motivations behind The Balkan Kitchen: to preserve these stories, but also to tell a fuller story about the Balkans through food — one that acknowledges our complex histories without reducing the region to them, and celebrates the creativity, cultural exchange and everyday life that have shaped our culinary traditions.

When you write about Balkan food, where does the Macedonian kitchen sit for you — as its own world, as part of a wider Balkan table, or as something that constantly moves between the two?

For me, it is both. The Macedonian kitchen has its own distinctive character, shaped by the landscapes, ingredients, customs and histories of the people who live there. It is also part of a wider regional culinary world in which people, ingredients, techniques and ideas have been moving across what are now national borders for centuries.

I grew up Macedonian, and that heritage is fundamental to how I understand food. The flavours I grew up with, the dishes my family cooked and the traditions I inherited are not interchangeable with someone else's experience. They are specific to my family and to the places that shaped us.

But when I began researching the history of these dishes, I also encountered connections that challenged the idea that culinary traditions can be neatly contained within modern national boundaries.

I don't see any contradiction here. Recognising that a dish has a particular place in Macedonian culture doesn't require us to deny its wider history, just as acknowledging regional connections doesn't diminish its significance to Macedonians.

We sometimes approach food as though acknowledging a shared history means surrendering something of our own. I see it differently. Understanding those connections gives us a much richer picture of our own beautiful culinary heritage, not a lesser one.

So much Macedonian culture survives through women's hands rather than official archives — ajvar-making, zelnik, tavče gravče, preserves, funeral and feast foods. What did the women in your family teach you that no written recipe could capture?

They taught me everything - but most importantly that a recipe is only one part of knowing how to cook. There is knowledge held in hands: how the dough should feel, how thin to stretch it, how much filling it needs, when something is ready without having to look at a clock. There is the ability to judge by touch, smell and instinct, developed through years of repetition.

My grandmother was extraordinary in the kitchen. As is my mother. My grandmother could produce the most wonderful food seemingly without effort, although it took me much longer to appreciate just how much skill and experience lay behind that apparent ease. Her instructions were not always precise, and many of her recipes had to be reconstructed from memory, conversations with my mother and my own attempts to recreate the dishes I remembered.

The women in my family showed me how food becomes part of the way we care for one another: how we prepare for a celebration, feed a family, welcome guests or keep someone close to us through a dish they love.

These forms of knowledge - much more profound than culinary technique - are particularly vulnerable because they are so often transmitted through everyday practice rather than formally documented. When the person who holds that knowledge is gone, you may discover that you have lost far more than a recipe. Part of what I wanted to do with my book was to record some of that knowledge before it disappeared, while recognising that no written recipe can fully reproduce the hands, judgement and experience of the person who first taught it to us.

When The Balkan Kitchen won the James Beard Award for Best International Book, did it feel like recognition for you personally, for Balkan cuisine broadly, or for Macedonian food traditions that are still often underrepresented?

I believe it was important on many levels. It was deeply personal, of course. Writing a book is an enormous undertaking, and to have that work recognised in such a significant way on the international culinary stage was incredibly moving and humbling.

But it also felt bigger than me. I had spent years researching and writing about a region that is too often represented internationally through a narrow set of historical and political narratives, frequently reduced to conflict, instability and negative stereotypes. I wanted to tell a different, more complex story: one that recognises our difficult histories without allowing them to define us, and that celebrates the beauty, richness, creativity and cultural depth of our culinary traditions.

I cannot claim that the award was specifically a recognition of Macedonian food, because The Balkan Kitchen explores a much wider regional landscape. But I hope that bringing these traditions to a broader international readership creates more space for Macedonian food to be encountered, understood and appreciated in its own right, as part of a distinctive culinary culture with its own histories and traditions.

For me, visibility matters, but so does the way we tell our stories — and the assumptions we challenge in doing so. I wanted to move beyond presenting Balkan food as an unfamiliar collection of recipes, or as a cuisine defined primarily by scarcity and survival, and instead explore the people, histories and cultural exchanges that have shaped it. I'm particularly encouraged by the interest the book has helped generate in Balkan cuisine, and by the conversations it has opened up around the region's culinary heritage.

The award was deeply meaningful not only as recognition of my work, but also because it helped bring these stories to a wider audience, inviting people to look beyond familiar stereotypes and discover a region and culinary cultures that deserve to be understood on their own terms.

Because the book is called The Balkan Kitchen, how did you make sure Macedonian food did not disappear inside the broader “Balkan” category?

By approaching the subject through meticulous, multidisciplinary research, with specificity and a genuine commitment to the individual stories behind the food. A regional scope for a book should not mean that the differences between places, communities and families disappear. Quite the opposite: a wider perspective should help us understand the extraordinary diversity across the region more clearly.

Macedonian food is part of the book not as a token representation of one country within a larger collection, but as an integral part of the region's long, complex and interconnected culinary history. The recipes, ingredients, techniques and customs all have their own contexts, and I wanted to give those contexts the care and attention they deserve.

The Balkans are not a single, uniform culinary culture. There are enormous differences across the region, and often within individual countries. At the same time, there are shared dishes and techniques whose histories cannot be understood solely through the lens of present-day national borders. The challenge is to acknowledge both realities without allowing one to flatten, or worse, erase the other.

The book's title reflects the geographical, historical and cultural scope of my research; it doesn't suggest that Macedonian food is any less distinct or important. I see the regional perspective as a way of understanding Macedonian cuisine, alongside the other national and local food traditions explored in the book, within a broader historical and cultural context. For me, recognising the connections between these cuisines does not diminish their individual identities. It allows us to understand more fully what makes each of them distinctive, as well as the histories and exchanges that connect them.

What is one Macedonian dish you wish the world understood more deeply — not just as a recipe, but as a story about who Macedonians are?

It has to be pita — or, depending on where you are in Macedonia and the particular preparation, zelnik or komat. For me, these dishes embody the extraordinary skill involved in working with dough in our cuisine. I associate them strongly with the cooking and baking I grew up around, and they can tell us a great deal about the knowledge and experience that underpin our home cooking.

There is something remarkable about transforming simple ingredients — flour, water, a little fat and a filling that might reflect the season or what a household has available — into something so generous and satisfying. Working the dough takes practice; the type of flour, the filling and even the way the pastry is shaped vary from family to family. Pita always carries the unique imprint of the person who made it for you and it is a dish where technique, memory, individual expression and love come together so beautifully.

But - I would resist the idea that any single dish can ever tell the full story of who Macedonians are. No cuisine can be reduced to one recipe, and no people have a single, fixed identity. However, dishes such as pita offer a useful entry point into understanding the everyday creativity, hospitality, comfort and accumulated knowledge that shape a culinary culture. They also speak to the love and care invested in feeding the people around us.

What interests me most is not simply whether someone abroad learns to make pita, but whether they become curious about the people who have made it, adapted it and passed it down.

That is when a recipe, a dish, becomes a story — and when food can help us understand a people and their culture more deeply.
