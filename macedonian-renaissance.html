Figures of Devotion

Four works of Macedonian sacred art reveal how painters shaped grief, movement, and devotion.

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Nerezi

At Saint Panteleimon in Gorno Nerezi, the Virgin draws the dead Christ toward her until their faces touch. Saint John bends to hold his hand. The mourners crowd close. Their grief is carried by the weight of a head, the curve of a back, and the effort of holding a body that can no longer support itself.

The church was built in 1164 by Alexios Komnenos, a nephew of Emperor John II Komnenos. The patron belonged to the Byzantine imperial family; the painters’ names have not survived. Their Lamentation occupies a wall in a small church, bringing the painted mourners close to the congregation.

The Virgin’s dark mantle forms a broad curve around Christ’s head and shoulders. John’s bent torso continues the downward movement. Above them, the bare slopes repeat the inclinations of the bodies. Almost everything directs attention toward the pale, horizontal figure of Christ. There is no elaborate architecture or distant view to draw the eye away.

Against the blue background, each contact is clear. Mary presses her cheek against her son’s. John brings Christ’s hand toward his face. The hands cradle and lift, giving grief the familiar actions of caring for someone. On the church wall, these gestures belong to a larger account of Christ’s life, seen by people gathered for worship.

Kurbinovo

At Saint George in Kurbinovo, near Lake Prespa, Gabriel seems caught midway through a step. His arm reaches toward the Virgin in the Annunciation, his wings rise behind him, and the fabric around his legs opens into long, looping folds. His small head and extended body make the stride seem longer still.

An inscription records the beginning of the church’s decoration on April 25, 1191, twenty-seven years after Nerezi. The building is a simple rectangle with a semicircular apse. Inside, the painters used its walls for figures whose gestures and garments reach well beyond the contours of their bodies.

Around Gabriel’s lower legs, pale edges twist outward, double back, and narrow into points. Some folds describe the limbs beneath them; others flare into the surrounding blue. The garment seems still to be responding to his arrival. His face remains composed, with his attention directed toward the Virgin.

At Nerezi, the mourners bend inward around Christ. Here, the outstretched arm and flying cloth carry the action across the wall. A photograph of Gabriel’s face alone misses much of this: the long passage from shoulder to foot, the slender legs, and the fabric that repeatedly breaks away from them.

Peribleptos

A century later, the painters Michael Astrapas and Eutychios filled the Virgin Peribleptos in Ohrid with densely populated scenes. A painted inscription dates the church’s foundation to 1294–95 and names its patrons, Progonos Sgouros and his wife, Eudokia Komnene. The painters also left their names within the decoration.

In the Dormition, the Virgin lies on a bier surrounded by mourners. Behind her, Christ holds her soul as a small, swaddled figure. Angels crowd above him, while apostles approach on clouds higher up. Several moments of the story gather around the still body at the painting’s base.

The mass of heads and halos separates into individual responses. One man bends low over the bier; others turn toward the Virgin or lean forward from behind their companions. People peer from openings in the buildings. Where Nerezi concentrates grief in a few bodies touching Christ, this scene brings a whole assembly to Mary’s death.

Pale walls and projecting roofs divide the gathering into smaller groups. Above the bier, the angels form a steep central column. These shapes hold the crowd together and lead the eye upward toward Christ and the soul he carries. Below them, the Virgin’s dark garment remains clearly outlined against the light cloth of the bed.

Dicho Zograf

Dicho Zograf’s Mother of God Pantonhara brings this encounter with sacred figures down to the scale of an individual panel. Painted in 1844 for Saint John at Kaneo, it is now in Ohrid’s Icon Gallery. The Virgin holds the Christ Child on her left arm. Both wear crowns; gold ornament spreads across her mantle, with a green garment beneath.

Dicho trained with the painters Mihail of Samarina and his son before establishing his own group in Tresonče. Working for church communities under Ottoman rule, he belonged to a workshop culture supported by commissions. The Pantonhara is one of his early signed works.

The crowns, scepters, and patterned borders give the panel a richness quite different from Nerezi’s spare landscape. Dicho separates the cloth with crisp edges and repeated ornament. Across the cheeks and around the eyes, however, shadow changes gradually into light. The faces appear soft amid the densely worked clothing.

The Virgin and Child were familiar subjects, with established poses, attributes, and inscriptions. Dicho’s choices emerge in the width of a face, the shape of an eyebrow, and the spacing of the ornaments. The panel’s narrow border holds these details close together. After the crowded walls of Peribleptos, the smallest changes in a cheek or eyelid become easier to follow.
