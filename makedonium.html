Architecture | Kruševo1974

Makedonium

A memorial imagined
through space, light, and sound.

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski
The building
and its possibilities

Above Kruševo, a white dome opens toward the landscape. Its windows reach out from the curved enclosure; inside, reliefs, colored glass, and music bring different forms of memory into one room. Makedonium opened on August 2, 1974. The architect was Iskra Grabuloska. The sculptor was Jordan Grabuloski.1,5

Their drawings reveal a larger ambition: a memorial that would teach through movement, images, and sound, including a projection room beneath the dome. Following that ambition through the archive changes how the building is seen. Each sheet records a possibility; each built space records a decision.8

On a sheet of tracing paper dated 1971, Makedonium is still an argument in felt-tip pen and pencil. Rounded forms cluster around a center. Lines overlap, double back, and leave the geometry unresolved. The sketch is now in the Museum of Modern Art, a gift from the Grabul family archive. It records a moment when the monument above Kruševo could still become something else.2

A shared authorship

Jordan’s career had already crossed the boundary between sculpture and architecture. He joined the artistic group Denes in 1953. Its membership included painters and architects, among them Borko Lazeski and Janko Konstantinov, whose Post Office in Skopje would become another major work of Macedonian modernism. The group argued for close collaboration between architecture, painting, and sculpture.5,12

Makedonium made that collaboration physical. Mazev’s ceramic work belongs to the outdoor setting; Lazeski’s stained glass changes the interior light; Jordan’s reliefs occupy the window recesses. Iskra’s architecture gives these works their relationships and the visitor a route between them.5

Credit needs to remain exact. The building is frequently reduced to Jordan’s name, or to a photograph with no authors at all. Art historian Raino Isto identifies Iskra as its architect and notes that the monument’s plaque distinguishes her role from Jordan’s as sculptor. Those names should remain together whenever the building is reproduced.7

The argument over abstraction

The process was contentious. Surviving records discussed in Isto’s archival study show sustained disagreement between the artists and the advisory bodies supervising the memorial. How clearly should the work narrate history? How much could an abstract form communicate? The arguments accompanied a design that changed between selection and completion.7

An unrealized programme

The room
below the dome.

The most revealing space may be the one visitors never received. A projection room was planned beneath the dome, with a spiral connection between levels. Archival research by Yane Calovski and Hristina Ivanoska describes slide projectors, cylindrical seating, and a central hollow support associated with a proposed cybernetic sculpture. The complete scheme remained unrealized.8

The section gives the lower space an outline and a relationship to the room above. Read it from ground level down: a second audience, a different way of teaching, and a programme that the completed monument never fully received.

Calovski returned to the unrealized room in Makedonium: Dramaturgy of the Unfinished, developed with Ivanoska. The project considers the space as a place for film, slides, discussion, and reflection. Their work carries the archive into contemporary art, allowing the unbuilt programme to remain available for thought.10

Ilinden is the Macedonian name for St. Elijah’s Day, August 2. In 1903, an uprising against Ottoman rule established a short-lived republic in Kruševo, led by Nikola Karev. Ottoman forces suppressed it after roughly ten days. Its brief existence became a foundational episode in the history of Macedonian statehood.23

The same date acquired a second meaning in 1944, when the first session of the Anti-Fascist Assembly for the National Liberation of Macedonia, known as ASNOM, laid the foundations of the Macedonian state within Yugoslavia. When Makedonium opened on August 2, 1974, the occasion joined the seventy-first anniversary of the uprising to the thirtieth anniversary of ASNOM.6,14

That double dedication matters. A visitor arrives at a memorial built in socialist Macedonia to connect an earlier revolution with the antifascist struggle and the state that followed. Its historical subject reaches backward; its architectural language belongs to a society actively deciding what the future should look like.

How the ground prepares you

The approach is part of the work. The museum describes four stages: broken chains, a crypt, an outdoor classroom, and the dome. The chains introduce release from captivity. The crypt’s fifty-eight inscribed elements record revolutionaries, events, and dates. Ahead, two curved walls carrying Mazev’s colored ceramic composition form the setting for an outdoor teaching space.1

This is an unusually precise arrangement of ways to remember. Sculpture introduces an idea without words. Inscriptions give it names. The classroom supplies a place to explain them. Only then does the route carry the visitor toward the dome. The building’s apparent strangeness has been prepared by a walk through increasingly explicit forms of public memory.

03
Inside the monument

The window
becomes a room.

The windows project outward from the rounded enclosure. From the exterior they give the building its extraordinary profile; from within, they create deep recesses. A window becomes a small spatial event, with its own direction, light, and sculptural surfaces. This is where a section drawing earns its place beside a photograph: it shows the thickness and depth behind the outline.

Borko Lazeski’s stained glass brings color into this largely white interior. In Boris Jurmovski’s photograph, blue glass is divided by an intricate framework above the reliefs. The color belongs to the light entering the room. As the visitor turns toward the glazing, the sculptural surface and the window are read together.13

The disagreement becomes tangible in Jordan’s interior reliefs. In Roberto Conte’s photograph of one, a projecting sphere is held among curved planes and angular fragments. A fractured surface extends behind it. The relief has depth, balance, and tension, but offers no easy sequence of historical figures for a visitor to identify.16

Abstraction makes a demand on public commemoration. A name and a date can be read immediately; a sphere surrounded by broken forms needs more time, and perhaps an explanation. The architecture places that demand beside actual views out of the building. The visitor moves between historical information, sculptural interpretation, and the landscape beyond.

Sound also belongs to the monument’s conception. The Museum of Kruševo identifies Toma Proshev’s oratorio Sun of the Ancient Land as part of the interior experience. Architecture, relief, glass, and music give the visitor different ways to enter the historical subject.1

The relief and the view through the window occupy the same field of attention. Looking outward is part of looking at the work.

An ongoing work

The conversation continues in Macedonia. In May 2026, the Institut français de Skopje hosted Ivanoska and Calovski in a programme that included their Makedonium film and discussion of monumental heritage. The building remains a subject for living artists, whose work asks fresh questions of its documents and decisions.18

Its fabric requires attention too. On July 22, 2026, a National Conservation Centre team inspected Lazeski’s stained-glass compositions in support of the Institute and Museum Prilep. The published report identified damage to the metal supporting structure and the glass, and called for urgent conservation measures. It described further investigation and documentation as the basis for a conservation and restoration project.11

That report returns us to the windows. Their outward reach produces the profile; their glass admits the color; their supports make both possible. Caring for them preserves an architectural idea as well as a material object.

Follow the drawings into the building and its unusual form becomes increasingly specific: a place to move through, a space for public teaching, an interior shaped by artists working across disciplines. The archive keeps the other possibilities in view.

Measurements that remain unverified

Published accounts disagree on site area and the number of openings. The article excludes these totals. A measured elevation, dated floor plan, original section sheet, structural specifications, and construction costs remain outstanding.

The museum gives an altitude above sea level; this is not the building’s height. The proposed lower room must not be presented as an intact, accessible hidden cinema.

Some secondary pages misidentify the August 2, 1944 ASNOM meeting as its second session. The State Archives establishes that it was the first.

The drawings here are archival reproductions. No architectural geometry has been invented or presented as a measured reconstruction.

02 Archive and publication record

The current article is based on published sources and visual inspection of reproduced material. It does not claim a site visit, interview, measured survey, or direct inspection of State Archives records.

Isto identifies fond 1066 at the State Archives, with Makedonium material in boxes 2–6. His finding references provide a route toward the proposal, sketches, and committee minutes; they have not been independently inspected here.

The artist-hosted PDF provided searchable text, but subsequent visual retrieval failed. The section was independently inspected through Neda Firfova’s photographed book spread, with printed pages 54–55 visible.

The 2022 exhibition Behind Makedonium is a further documented route toward family-held material. The remaining archival work should establish sheet titles, dates, revisions, and publication-quality reproductions.
