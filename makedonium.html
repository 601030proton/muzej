Architecture | Kruševo

Makedonium

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

The making of the Ilinden Monument, through its drawings, spaces, and works of art.

Opened
August 2, 1974
Architecture
Iskra Grabuloska
Sculpture
Jordan Grabuloski
Ceramic work
Petar Mazev
Stained glass
Borko Lazeski

The Ilinden Monument stands on Gumenje, above Kruševo. Opened on August 2, 1974, it brought together architecture by Iskra Grabuloska, sculpture by Jordan Grabuloski, ceramics by Petar Mazev, and stained glass by Borko Lazeski. The dome known as Makedonium is the last part of a larger composition: a route through the hillside, with places for commemoration, gathering, and teaching.1, 5

Its construction photograph records the work before the white surfaces were complete. Reinforcing steel rises around the curved enclosure; scaffolding follows the projecting window forms, and workers occupy the structure at several levels. The image provides a useful beginning for an account of a building whose finished appearance can conceal the labor of its making.4

The drawings preserve another part of that history. In the surviving sketch and competition perspective, the form is being tested on paper. Other documents record disagreements over its meaning and a lower room whose intended program was never completed. Read together, these materials describe a memorial developed through collaboration, revision, and negotiation.2, 3, 7

1903 / 1944 / 1974

Two Ilindens

The opening date joined two events separated by forty-one years. On Ilinden, St. Elijah’s Day, in 1903, an uprising against Ottoman rule established a republic in Kruševo under Nikola Karev. It survived for roughly ten days before Ottoman forces suppressed it. The episode became a central reference in accounts of Macedonian statehood.23

On August 2, 1944, the first session of the Anti-Fascist Assembly for the National Liberation of Macedonia, known as ASNOM, laid the foundations of the Macedonian state within Yugoslavia. Makedonium opened on the seventy-first anniversary of the uprising and the thirtieth anniversary of that assembly. Its dedication connected the Ilinden generation with the antifascist struggle of the Second World War.6, 14

The commission therefore carried more than one historical subject. It had to commemorate the dead, explain the relationship between two periods, and provide a public setting for their remembrance. The completed complex distributes those tasks across the ground before bringing them together within the dome.

MoMA holds two sheets from the Grabul family archive that allow part of the design process to be followed. The sketch dated 1971 is made in felt-tip pen and pencil on tracing paper. Its overlapping lines and rounded studies retain the provisional character of a working drawing. The competition perspective dated 1972 uses ink and charcoal, with a darker ground and a more clearly defined approach to the building.2, 3

The sheets have different purposes. One tests a form; the other presents an architectural setting. Neither should be treated as a measured record of the completed monument. Their value is in the decisions they preserve, including the relationship between the central volume, its openings, and the path leading toward it.

The commission

A collaboration in the landscape

Jordan Grabuloski’s work had long involved a close relationship between sculpture and its setting. In 1953 he joined Denes, a group whose members included painters and architects. Its manifesto advocated collaboration among architecture, painting, and sculpture; Borko Lazeski and Janko Konstantinov were also members. The group’s position helps place Makedonium within an existing discussion in Macedonian art.5, 12

At Kruševo, that collaboration extended across several kinds of work. Mazev supplied the ceramic composition for the outdoor classroom and Lazeski made the stained glass. Jordan’s reliefs became part of the interior. The architecture organized their positions and the movement between them. Its authorship belongs to Iskra Grabuloska, whose role is distinguished from Jordan’s as sculptor on the monument’s plaque, as art historian Raino Isto records.5, 7

The archive also documents sustained disagreement between the artists and the bodies supervising the commission. At issue was the public legibility of abstraction: whether a memorial to particular events should represent them through recognizable figures or through sculptural forms that required interpretation. These discussions accompanied changes to the project before its completion.7

The approach

The Museum of Kruševo describes four stages in the completed complex. Broken chains begin the route, followed by a crypt, an outdoor classroom, and the dome. The crypt contains fifty-eight inscribed elements recording revolutionaries, events, and dates. Two curved walls carrying Mazev’s ceramic work form the setting of the classroom.1

The sequence changes the visitor’s relationship to the historical subject. The broken chains present a sculptural image of release. Inscriptions introduce names and dates, while the classroom provides a place to sit and hear an explanation. The dome is reached after these different forms of public memory have been encountered along the approach.

Valentin Jeck’s photograph makes the outdoor arrangement particularly clear. Cylindrical seats occupy the foreground, the colored wall curves behind them, and the white building rises beyond. The composition places the monumental form in relation to seated bodies and a shared space. This part of the complex is essential to understanding the dome’s position on the hill.17

Inside the dome

Within the dome, the projecting windows become deep recesses. They carry the eye through the enclosure toward the landscape and establish distinct positions around the room. The exterior profile and the interior arrangement belong to the same system of openings.

Jordan’s eight reliefs give the lower recesses a sculptural depth of their own. In Roberto Conte’s photograph of one relief, a sphere projects among curved planes and angular fragments, with a fractured surface extending behind it. Its composition depends on the relationship between those parts, their shadows, and the white wall.1, 16

Lazeski’s stained glass brings a different treatment of surface and depth. In Boris Jurmovski’s interior photograph, the intricate framework of a blue window appears above the reliefs. The glass admits colored light into the enclosure, while the sculptural forms remain predominantly white. The window, its supporting structure, and the surfaces around it are read together.13

Music was also included in the interior’s conception. The museum identifies Toma Proshev’s oratorio Sun of the Ancient Land as part of the experience. The combination of relief, glass, and sound gave the memorial several means of addressing its historical subject within a single architectural space.1

The unbuilt program

The projection room

The intended program continued beneath the main room. Research by Yane Calovski and Hristina Ivanoska describes a proposed projection space connected to the dome by a spiral stair. Slide projectors and cylindrical seating were planned, along with a hollow central support associated with a proposed cybernetic sculpture. The complete scheme remained unrealized.8

A technical section reproduced in their artist book shows the relationship between the principal enclosure and the proposed lower space. The spread is visible in designer Neda Firfova’s portfolio. It records an architectural proposal, rather than establishing the basement’s present condition or accessibility.9

Calovski and Ivanoska returned to this material in Makedonium: Dramaturgy of the Unfinished. Their project examines the unrealized room as a setting for projected images, discussion, and reflection. The archival work gives this part of the design a continued presence, alongside the spaces that were completed and opened to visitors.10

The building after 1974

The building’s materials have their own continuing history. On July 22, 2026, a National Conservation Centre team inspected Lazeski’s stained-glass compositions in support of the Institute and Museum Prilep. Its published report identified damage to the glass and metal supports, called for urgent protective measures, and set out the need for further investigation and conservation documentation.11

The drawings, construction photographs, and surviving fabric describe different stages of the same work. The memorial opened in 1974; its interpretation, use, and conservation have continued since. The unbuilt projection room remains part of the record of its conception, while the approach, classroom, reliefs, and windows show how much of that conception was given a physical form.

Original sheets and a measured elevation remain to be obtained. Published accounts disagree on site area and window totals; these measurements are excluded here.
