The Many Faces of Nikola Martinoski

From a Skopje icon painter’s workshop to Bucharest and Paris, tracing the education and recurring subjects of Nikola Martinoski.

Before Nikola Martinoski went to Paris, he painted a landscape in the workshop of an icon painter in Skopje. The National Gallery dates Landscape to 1919 and describes it as probably his earliest surviving work. It is a useful place to begin with an artist usually introduced as a founder of modern Macedonian painting: a teenager learning to paint, with years of training still ahead of him.

Martinoski was born in Kruševo on August 18, 1903, and moved to Skopje as a child. By 1919, he and Tomo Vladimirski were working in the workshop of Dimitar Andonov Papradishki. His apprenticeship included making pictures after colored postcards. That modest exercise belongs to the same education as the encounter with icon painting: learning how an image is constructed by working through images already made.

The distance between Landscape and the Self-Portrait of 1949 reproduced here spans his professional education, his first exhibitions, a war, and the establishment of new art institutions in Macedonia. The self-portrait gives that history an individual subject. Martinoski repeatedly painted other people, especially women and children from Skopje’s poorer neighborhoods; here the painter himself enters the body of portraits through which he examined the human figure.

Between the two works came seven years at the School of Fine Arts in Bucharest, from 1920 to 1927. The National Gallery’s 2013 jubilee catalog records that he graduated as the best student of his year. His education there moved from the practical instruction of the workshop into sustained professional study. Two student paintings help make that development more concrete.

Still Life with a Skull belongs to the basic course he attended between 1920 and 1924. Its subject brings together objects for study, a different task from painting a landscape or a living sitter. The title also matters when looking at the still life reproduced later in this article, with its bottle, glass, and fruit. Still life remained part of his work long after his student years, alongside the portraits and scenes of motherhood for which he became better known.

The small painting In the Field marks a further stage in Bucharest. In its account of his early work, the National Gallery associates it with the new direction that began when he entered Camil Ressu’s special painting course in 1925. These student works give some substance to a period that can otherwise disappear into a sentence about European training. Martinoski had already spent years developing his practice before he encountered the Paris art world.

He stayed in Paris in 1927–1928, attending the Académie de la Grande Chaumière and the Académie Ranson. There he encountered the School of Paris and the work of artists associated with Expressionism and post-Cubism. Boris Petkovski treats the stay as a turning point. The effects became particularly apparent in the paintings Martinoski made after returning to Skopje at the end of 1928.

His first solo exhibition opened in Skopje in 1929. In the early 1930s, café life and prostitution supplied subjects for paintings in which figures were distorted and sometimes caricatured. A 2009 museum catalog describes that deformation as a way of intensifying the brutality of the subject. The angular bodies and exaggerated faces belong to this particular treatment of urban life; calling them modernist is only the beginning of an explanation.

The setting was important. The National Gallery describes the interwar emergence of modern Macedonian art within a political environment that denied Macedonian national, linguistic, and cultural identity. Martinoski and his contemporaries often trained elsewhere because that was where professional education was available. On returning, they worked with subjects drawn from their surroundings while developing an audience for forms of painting unfamiliar to much of that audience.

By the middle of the 1930s, Martinoski’s treatment of his figures had become gentler. He continued to find models in Skopje’s Roma neighborhoods, painting children, mothers, and wet nurses. The National Gallery’s catalog describes this phase as lyrical realism, followed by a stronger social realism between 1938 and 1941. Clothing, posture, and expression became ways of registering poverty and resignation. The people he painted remained recognizable individuals even when he exaggerated a feature or altered the proportions of a body.

A mother and child offered him a recurring composition within which to make those adjustments.

The seated woman holding a child reproduced above brings together two of Martinoski’s most persistent interests: the individual figure and the relationship between mother and child. He returned to this subject throughout his career. Its recurrence makes it possible to follow changes in his handling of a familiar arrangement, from the social realism of the late 1930s into the experiments of the postwar years.

Writing about these paintings, Mirjana Taleska describes mothers with firm bodies and carefully formed heads, often arranged in pyramidal compositions. Faces receive comparatively realistic treatment, while hands may be subtly deformed and painted more freely. That distinction gives the subject a formal interest beyond its emotional associations: different parts of one figure can be handled at different levels of precision. A mother and child offered him a recurring composition within which to make those adjustments.

The still life with bottle, glass, and fruit offers a change of subject beside it. Martinoski’s still lifes included flowers, fish, and game, sometimes painted on glass. His handling could emphasize saturated color, broad strokes, and the mass of objects. Set beside Still Life with a Skull, the later still-life practice shows how a subject introduced during his education continued to provide material for experiment. It also broadens an account of his work that can become too exclusively concerned with faces.

After the war, Martinoski continued to paint familiar subjects while changing his treatment of them. Petkovski describes the years from 1951 to 1960 as a discontinuous period of attempts to recover a personal language. Drawings from the 1950s brought expressionist distortion together with symbolism, surrealism, and references to Byzantine art. Angular constructions became prominent in the later years of the decade, followed by more curved and elongated forms. These changes complicate any account that treats his mature style as a single settled achievement.

The portrait of an elderly bearded man reproduced here returns attention to the individual sitter. Portraiture occupied Martinoski across those changes in style, as did his own likeness. Blaže Koneski recalled the concentration with which he studied a model, looking down only intermittently to check the drawing. The recollection describes an acquired coordination of observation and movement, developed through decades of work. His materials ranged from oil on canvas and cardboard to tempera, pencil, chalk, and oil on glass.

His public responsibilities grew alongside his practice. After the liberation of Skopje, he helped establish and led the school now known as the School of Applied Arts, then directed the Art Gallery in Skopje. He also served as president of the Association of Fine Artists of Macedonia and became a regular member of MANU in 1967. These roles helped establish teaching, exhibition, and professional structures for artists in Macedonia. He remained active until his death in Skopje on February 7, 1973.

The surviving work gives an incomplete account of that career. Paintings were damaged or lost during the Second World War, the flood of 1962, and the Skopje earthquake of 1963. Martinoski also responded to the earthquake in drawings and paintings, returning to the human figure under circumstances very different from a portrait sitting. His works are held in Kruševo and Skopje, as well as in other public and private collections, and exhibitions have continued to bring less familiar material into view.

In 2017, the National Gallery’s exhibition Nikola Martinoski—Beginnings, 1919–1929 assembled thirty images tracing his earliest development. Among them was Landscape, the small beginning from Papradishki’s workshop. The exhibition also used photographic reproductions of works too damaged to travel to Skopje. For a painter so often introduced through his established reputation, it offered something more useful: the chance to follow the work before that reputation existed.
