Nikola Martinoski: Becoming a Painter

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Nikola Martinoski trained in Skopje, Bucharest, and Paris and found many of his subjects in Skopje’s Roma neighborhoods.

As a teenager in Skopje, Nikola Martinoski learned to paint in an icon painter’s workshop. The National Gallery dates his Landscape to 1919 and considers it probably his earliest surviving work.

Martinoski was born in Kruševo on August 18, 1903, and moved to Skopje as a child. By 1919, he and Tomo Vladimirski were working in the studio of Dimitar Andonov Papradishki. One exercise was to copy color postcards by hand.

Martinoski attended the School of Fine Arts in Bucharest from 1920 to 1927. According to the National Gallery’s 2013 jubilee catalog, he graduated at the top of his class.

He painted Still Life with a Skull during his basic course, which ran from 1920 to 1924. The National Gallery associates In the Field with a change in direction after he entered Camil Ressu’s painting course in 1925.

In 1927–1928, Martinoski attended the Académie de la Grande Chaumière and the Académie Ranson in Paris. He encountered the School of Paris and work associated with Expressionism and post-Cubism. Art historian Boris Petkovski identifies the stay as a turning point, particularly for the paintings made after Martinoski returned to Skopje in late 1928.

His first solo exhibition opened in Skopje in 1929. In the early 1930s, Martinoski painted cafés and scenes of prostitution. In a 2009 museum catalog, the angular bodies and exaggerated faces are read as a way of emphasizing the brutality of that part of urban life.

The National Gallery situates the emergence of modern Macedonian art in an interwar political environment that denied Macedonian national, linguistic, and cultural identity. Martinoski and several contemporaries trained abroad and drew on local subjects after returning.

By the mid-1930s, Martinoski was painting children, mothers, and wet nurses from Skopje’s Roma neighborhoods. The National Gallery describes these paintings as lyrical realism and identifies a stronger social realism in the work of 1938–1941. Clothing, posture, and expression register the poverty of his sitters.

He often painted faces with more precision than hands.

Martinoski painted mothers with children throughout his career. Mirjana Taleska describes the figures’ broad bodies and carefully modeled heads, and notes that they often form a pyramid.

He often painted faces with more precision than hands. The hands could be distorted or painted in looser strokes.

Martinoski also painted still lifes of fruit, flowers, fish, and game, sometimes working in oil on glass. The bottle, glass, and fruit in the still life shown here are painted in broad strokes and saturated color.

Petkovski describes the years 1951–1960 as a period of uneven experimentation. Martinoski’s drawings combined expressionist distortion with symbolism, surrealism, and references to Byzantine art. Late in the decade, angular forms became more prominent before giving way to longer curves.

Martinoski continued to paint portraits and self-portraits as his style changed. Blaže Koneski recalled watching him draw a sitter, looking down only occasionally to check the page. Martinoski worked in oil on canvas and cardboard, as well as in tempera, pencil, chalk, and oil on glass.

After the liberation of Skopje, Martinoski helped establish and led what is now the School of Applied Arts. He later directed the Art Gallery in Skopje and served as president of the Association of Fine Artists of Macedonia. He became a regular member of the Macedonian Academy of Sciences and Arts in 1967 and died in Skopje on February 7, 1973.

Paintings were damaged or lost during World War II, the flood of 1962, and the Skopje earthquake of 1963. Martinoski responded to the earthquake in drawings and paintings. His works are held in collections in Kruševo, Skopje, and elsewhere.

In 2017, the National Gallery assembled thirty images for Nikola Martinoski—Beginnings, 1919–1929. The exhibition included Landscape and photographs of works too damaged to travel to Skopje.
