Nikola Martinoski in Paris

Words Kiril Penušliski

Paris, 1927–1928

Nikola Martinoski spent about a year in Paris, from late 1927 to late 1928. While there, he attended two art schools, the Académie de la Grande Chaumière and the Académie Ranson. He also spent some time working in the private studios of colleagues and several French artists.

Paris was the center of modern art in the first half of the twentieth century. Martinoski himself said that the time he spent there was the most productive period of his education. The modern art he encountered in France shaped his thinking about how he wanted his own painting to develop.

Martinoski took part in several group exhibitions during his stay in Paris, though we cannot identify them all. Many of the paintings he made that year are now lost, including the works he showed in the exhibition of Yugoslav artists at the Institute of Slavic Studies in February 1928. The titles of his three canvases in that exhibition tell us little about them. Bridge over the Seine must have been a cityscape. Interior may have been a composition with figures in an indoor setting, and Figure was most likely a nude. We may suppose that they date from his first months in France. As early works from his Paris period, they must still have been close in style to the academic painting of the Bucharest school he had attended for the previous seven years.

Despite these losses, we have a group of paintings that we know were made in 1928, while Martinoski was in France. At the center of this Paris group are several works on which he added the city to his signature. Others carry no place name but are clearly linked to his stay by their style. Six paintings by Martinoski can now be identified as having been made in Paris.

Portrait of a Woman is a small painting in oil on canvas, measuring 39.5 × 31 cm and signed Martin/1928/Paris at lower left. It shows a woman’s head in profile. She has short dark hair, a prominent nose, and full lips. The outline of her pale face stands out against a dark, simply painted background that gives little sense of where she is. Martinoski adds a patch of light at the back of her head to keep its outline from disappearing into the darkness. The painting gives the impression that he worked directly, without preparation, with the model in front of him. Though he reduces her features to quick strokes, we can tell that he painted a person who sat for him.

There is no visible preparatory sketch of the head, nor is there a drawn outline of the head and shoulders like the one in Female Nude, Martinoski’s graduation painting from a few months earlier. The painting also differs clearly from every head study he made while studying in Bucharest. In contrast to Lazy Stoica, Head of a Little Boy, and Boy in Profile, strong strokes replace finely graded tones and delicate modeling. The paint is dense and the brushwork vigorous, giving the whole surface of the canvas a palpable texture. The only earlier work that comes close in its handling is the small painting In the Field. Portrait of a Woman seems to pick up where that painting left off, skipping over the years in Camil Ressu’s studio while drawing on the technique and experience Martinoski gained in his last years in Bucharest. He tackles the problems of painting here with a freedom we have never seen in his earlier canvases.

For all that freedom, it is hard to detect any direct influence from the modern work Martinoski saw in Paris. I therefore think he made the portrait in his first few months there, while still at the Académie de la Grande Chaumière and before moving to the Académie Ranson. That would put it between late 1927 and the early months of 1928. The sitter was most likely one of the models who posed at the Grande Chaumière.

It is possible that the same model also posed for Lazar Ličenoski. After studying in Belgrade, Ličenoski received a scholarship to study fresco painting in Paris in Marcel-Lenoir’s studio. He attended André Lhote’s studio at the Grande Chaumière, apparently at the same time as Martinoski. One of Ličenoski’s Paris paintings is Female Nude, an oil on canvas measuring 42 × 30 cm, signed Ličenoski/1927 at lower right. It shows the bare torso of a young woman wearing a sheer scarf or blouse over her lower body and a red necklace. Her short dark hair is cut in a recognizable 1920s style, and she has a slight chin, full lips with a marked curve in the lower lip, a distinctive nose, and arched eyebrows. Ličenoski and Martinoski paint very differently, yet the features of the two sitters are remarkably close.

Another study of a woman’s head from this period is in the collection of the Nikola Martinoski Gallery in Kruševo. It, too, bears the name of the city in its signature, Paris N Martin 1928, midway up the canvas near the right edge.

Where Portrait of a Woman was painted in quick, restless expressionist strokes, Head of a Woman takes an altogether different approach to modeling. Again, we see a young woman with short dark hair, this time in three-quarter view. She wears a sleeveless dress or blouse, a necklace, and long earrings. Her pensive gaze is directed beyond the picture, and the background suggests the space she occupies. The drawing is visible on the canvas, in the nose, lips, and eyes, in the outline of the head and shoulders, and in the details of the clothes.

The modeling sets this painting apart from Portrait of a Woman. Martinoski applies paint with a broad brush or a palette knife. He constructs the sitter’s face from interlocking planes in a manner that follows Synthetic Cubism. The forms are solid and three-dimensional. The picture is also held together by a consistent range of tones. The color of the dress and of the background corresponds to the shadows on the sitter.

Portrait of a Woman retains something of the academy, especially in the placement of the head. The language of Head of a Woman looks toward modern painting in Paris. Boris Petkovski links it to the return to reality and a renewed taste for the classical in André Derain’s work, a turn still informed by Derain’s experience of Fauvism and Cubist and Constructivist painting. In other words, Martinoski treats a classical subject with a modern pictorial language. We may safely place this canvas after Portrait of a Woman.

Female Nude with a Hat, in the collection of the Museum in Smederevo, belongs to the same period. As Petkovski describes it, the seated woman’s body has full, compact forms. Broad, intermittently broken contours separate the figure from the ground. Her pose is convincing, with little sensuality or suggestion of emotion in the face. The rendering of the body has nothing in common with Martinoski’s surviving student nudes from Bucharest.

The influence of modern French painting on his work in this period is especially clear if we compare two still lifes. Both feature a large wine bottle, a glass, an oval metal tray, and several apples. The first, which I will call Still Life with a Bottle, Glass, and Fruit 1 (oil on cardboard, 73 × 51.4 cm), is unsigned and in a private collection in the Netherlands. The second, Still Life with a Bottle, Glass, and Fruit 2 (oil on canvas, 49.4 × 33.5 cm), is in a private collection in Skopje. It is signed Martin/1928 at lower right.

Still Life with a Bottle, Glass, and Fruit 1 is a realist painting and one of his finest early works in the genre. It shows a bottle of red Bordeaux, a full glass of wine, a metal tray, several apples, and a knife. One apple is half peeled. A cloth in the background is darker than the white cloth on the small table that holds the objects. The tray mirrors one of the apples, the glass of wine, and the bottle label.

In the second painting we find the same objects: several apples, now green, in an oval tray, and a glass full of red wine by a dark bottle. The glass and the bottle occupy the same positions in both paintings. In the second, however, Martinoski replaces the reflection of the wine label with a patch of pure white and signs the painting on it. It is the only patch of pure white in the composition, but its position precisely follows the reflection of the label in the first painting. The artist’s signature takes the place of the name of the wine. His repetition and revision of this one detail show which painting came second.

Stylistically, the two are quite different. The later painting shows the direct influence of the Cubist work Martinoski encountered in Paris. We can see it in the placement of the objects, in the way he renders them on the canvas, and in the changes in perspective that bring out their geometry. The painting in the Netherlands is a faithful treatment of a classical genre. Its objects are rendered realistically and the scene is softly and evenly lit.

Chess may date from the end of Martinoski’s stay in Paris or from immediately after his return to Macedonia. It certainly dates from the second half of 1928. The painting measures 50 × 73 cm and is signed N. S. Martinoski 1928 at lower right. It shows three men around a table set with a white cloth, with a chessboard at the center of the picture. Petkovski describes massive, blocklike and cylindrical figures with emphatically expressive forms. Their faces are at once ingenuous and brutal. Petkovski uses the term “Mongoloid” for their exaggerated features. The distortion of the figures makes the whole composition unsettling and provocative.

The man on the left in a blue shirt and the man on the right seem to be playing. The third man is a kibitzer. He leans toward the player on the left to whisper something, while the man on the right seems to reach toward the board to make a move. The board has no pieces. But for all its distortion, it has the correct sixty-four squares and is set properly between the players, with a white square in the lower right corner from each player’s point of view. Martinoski’s omission of the pieces lets the viewer’s eye follow the circle of gestures around the board. This is one of the few paintings in which his figures make up a truly Mannerist composition. By leaving off the pieces, the most important objects on a chessboard, he makes the board itself more important. The chessboard is now the center of the world.

Romani Woman Drinking Coffee dates from late 1928, probably just after Martinoski returned to Skopje. It is an oil on canvas, 90 × 47 cm, signed Martin/928, now in the collection of the Museum of the City of Skopje. A barefoot woman sits on the ground with her hair covered by a scarf. She wears an oversized black coat and light-colored trousers and holds a little cup in her right hand. Her pose and the setting strongly recall Homeless, a drawing dated to the same year.

The Frescoes at the Okean Café

Martinoski’s nudes on the walls of the Okean café date from the mid-1930s. The café was demolished a few years after the Second World War, in 1947 or 1948. A few black-and-white photographs of the interior and some blurred newspaper reproductions have survived, giving us a limited view of these paintings.

The café occupied the ground floor of a house Martinoski owned at 18 Kornelije Stanković Street. The idea of decorating the interior with wall paintings came from the man who ran it at the time. A report published in Vreme late in 1936 recounts:

Mr. Martinoski made these fresco compositions last autumn at the request of the tenant of the Okean, whose business had been doing poorly for two years. Customers came less and less often, and those who did come were reluctant to stay. The proprietor, Dane, thought for a long time about the reason. Setting aside the crisis and the modern age, when a man could find a “companion” even out on the street, he hit upon an important idea. […] He asked Mr. Martinoski to decorate the walls somehow, and the painter spent a full fifteen days painting the interior.

Martinoski seems to have had several assistants. Vasilije Popović-Cico may have been one of them. No preparatory sketches have survived, and there may never have been any. He worked in fresco secco, painting on dry plaster. According to Jaroslav Kratina, the technique suited Martinoski because it let him work much as he would at an easel: he could make corrections and develop a more intimate relationship with his materials. Kratina also supposed that he used a specially prepared emulsion as a binder, colloquially called “young casein.” From eyewitness accounts we know that he mainly used shades of yellow and brown ocher, strong greens, and various reds and blues. The lightest passages were painted in yellow ocher. The reds were dark, the greens were used for shadows, and the background was generally an intense blue.

The titles of the decorations have not survived. Petkovski mistakenly treated words from a description in Vreme as the names of some of the murals, including Feasts, Orgies, and Girls. The most detailed account is by B. Antić in the daily Politika of April 15, 1936:

The new decorations at the Okean were painted in the pure expressionist manner that otherwise characterizes Mr. Martinoski’s painting. Each picture gave form to an idea of the painter’s and sought to show the intense inner lives of men and women in different situations and relationships. Across the entire wall above the platform where the mixed ensemble plays, there is a bacchanal set outdoors, nearly three meters by two. Men and women, who might be actors from antiquity, roll about on the grass, almost drunk, in the strangest tangle of bodies, arms, and legs. Wine pours freely from overflowing goblets into the mouths of those still able to drink it. In the distance, half-drunk figures make strange movements. The men and women are completely naked and life-size.

To the right is a sleeping woman. Her head rests against her breast. A strange animal passes over her and touches her with its tail, and the woman feels desire in her sleep. Further on there is a Romanian circle dance. To the left of the entrance is the strangest picture in this still stranger gallery: a great knot of crossed arms and legs. It is meant to show a man and woman at rest, dreaming of happiness. Further on is a fine painting, both in execution and conception, of a woman’s maturity. Another large composition shows the love of the swan. In this way Mr. Martinoski painted thirteen large pictures around the walls of the café. Several are so successful that they could readily be shown in an exhibition. All the figures, male and female, are completely nude. The distortion of line owes more to expressionist painting than to any intention of arousing erotic feelings in the people who frequent the café.

Antić’s account lets us identify six compositions: the large bacchanal above the music platform, measuring three by two meters; a woman asleep beside an animal; a Romanian circle dance; a man and woman dreaming of happiness; a composition about a woman’s maturity; and a scene from the Greek myth of Leda and the Swan. Antić gives the total as thirteen, while other contemporary accounts of Martinoski’s work at the Okean count twelve.

Soon after their completion, the frescoes were reported to the police as pornographic and offensive to public morals. Sometime in the autumn of 1935 Martinoski had to defend them before Emil Putnik, then head of the criminal police in Skopje. Putnik, described in the press as an educated, progressive man who loved art, looked closely at the walls of the Okean and concluded that the paintings were works of art and could not be considered pornography offensive to public morals.

Several months later, in the second half of March 1936, a public health commission inspecting the premises again remarked on the unusual wall decorations. The police administration stated in its report that “the walls of this establishment are covered with pornographic pictures.” A two-person commission was formed to assess the works. Its members were a painter, Dragutin Inkiostri-Medenjak, who was then employed in the education department of the Banovina administration, and a police official.

The commission’s verdict was equivocal. It agreed that the frescoes were unquestionably works of art and regretted that, as such, they were on the walls of a nightspot. Yet because of where they had been painted, it held that if they were to remain as wall decorations, they could not remain in their original condition. Certain things, it concluded, would have to be altered to bring them into line with public morals.

Martinoski complied with the ruling and made extensive changes to the compositions. He removed the male and female figures that might appear “dangerous” under a strict interpretation of morality. He toned down the gestures and facial expressions of others. Antić described him as removing any sinister expressions that could, in the commission’s view, pose a moral danger to visitors. Dealing with the nudity was simpler: he covered it up. Nymphs, drunkards, ballerinas, and “mature women” were all given something to make them decent. Some acquired drawers with lace at the hems, in the fashion of 1890. Others were given stylish modern slips. He tied a towel around the waist of each male figure and masked some of the more expressive, stylized ballet scenes with leaves and plants.

When the commission looked at the paintings again, it found them decent after the changes but still incompatible with public morals in that particular venue. By Skopje police administration ruling no. 27762 of April 10, 1937, Martinoski was ordered to pay a fine of fifty dinars or serve one day in jail.

On April 14, 1937, Martinoski submitted a written appeal to the Banovina administration in Skopje. He cited the state constitution, which guaranteed freedom and protection for the sciences and arts, and argued that his frescoes were works of art. In the account of his appeal, he said:

It makes no difference whether a work of art is on a canvas or on a wall, whether it is in a drawing room or in a café. It remains a work of art. […] There is no place here for a formula, and a work like this cannot be judged on the basis of regulations for the interior of premises or of police orders.

Martinoski questioned the artistic competence of the commission that had decided to clothe the nudes. He also denied that the police administration had the right to judge whether a work was art or pornography. Such a question, he said, could be assessed by a panel of artists with the requisite expertise. He was convinced of the soundness of his work and resolved to serve the day in jail in preference to paying the fifty dinars if the ruling stood.

Unfortunately, we do not know the outcome. Skopje’s daily newspapers reported nothing about the proceedings or the controversy surrounding the Okean frescoes.

The surviving photographs show that most of the murals depicted life-size human figures in wooden frames attached directly to the walls. Reproductions appeared in Vreme on December 6, 1936, and in Politika on April 15, 1937. In all, there is a photographic record of six of the Okean frescoes.

Only one of the murals in the photographs can be identified with certainty as a work in Antić’s account: the sleeping woman touched by an animal. The photograph suggests that it was painted across two adjoining walls. The wooden frame runs horizontally from left to right, then angles toward the upper right corner at the center of the photograph. A vertical line also runs down the middle of the mural, across the woman’s body below her abdomen.

The work is a reclining nude. The woman wears only a necklace, which suggests that the fresco was photographed before Martinoski made his corrections. The photograph is too indistinct for us to say whether she is outdoors or inside. There appears to be another framed picture above her head, possibly another female nude. If so, the painting within the painting suggests an interior.

The animal Antić mentions is next to the woman and seems to follow the outline of her body. Beginning at the knee of her raised right leg, it runs parallel to her, across her thigh and up to her right breast. We can see its head, an open mouth, and indications of eyes. It is hard to say whether it is a stylized snake, which would give the work an allegorical meaning, a dog, perhaps a dachshund, or an invented creature. The mural has distinct dark passages only in the woman’s hair, at the necklace, and between her legs.

This nude is unusual in Martinoski’s work. The woman’s body is powerfully built, her face is in profile, and her pose is suggestive. Petkovski relates the frescoes to Henri Matisse, but the woman’s features and the form of her strong body have more in common with some of Pablo Picasso’s works from the early 1920s.

I suspect that the long horizontal composition reproduced in Vreme and Politika is the café’s largest fresco, the painting three meters by two above the music platform. The blurred photographs reveal a complex arrangement of human figures. We cannot make out the details of their bodies or say exactly how they are placed. Are there two or three figures at the far left, perhaps two women and a man? Nor is it clear how much of the composition the images show. The Politika photograph shows that the mural continues to the right beyond the last woman visible in Vreme. We can see a succession of naked figures: a woman leaning against a man behind her, another suggestively spreading her legs toward the viewer, and a third who may be dancing. They all seem to be in a landscape. Antić’s description of a bacchanal fits what we see in the photographs.

One photograph of the interior shows two of Martinoski’s compositions on the same wall, separated by a supporting column. The nudes are smaller than life-size, and the compositions were painted at about mid-height on the café walls.

The mural on the left shows two naked women standing in a landscape. The woman on the left holds a piece of fruit, while the other seems to carry something above her head. The subject of the mural on the right is less clear. Are these abstract geometric forms or lovers in an embrace? At the center we can make out a leg and a buttock. The two round shapes above them may be the lovers’ heads.

The photograph shows no direct continuity between the images. The horizon in the landscape on the left does not line up with the one in the fresco on the right, and their subjects, at least in these two works, do not form a single scene.

Another photograph of the interior shows three figures in a landscape. The two on the left are clearly women. The sex of the figure on the right, in the farthest plane, is impossible to tell. From the lower left corner of the mural a dog emerges, apparently attacking the woman in the center.

One of the compositions reproduced in Politika perhaps best shows how suggestive the frescoes could be. It depicts three female nudes. Only the woman in the middle faces us. The seated woman to the left and the standing figure on the right turn their backs to the viewer. In the newspaper photograph, at least, the shape of the seated woman’s back resembles an erect penis.

For all their poor quality, the photographs show that Martinoski adapted his pictorial language to the venue and the wall-painting technique. These scenes simplify the forms into decorative patterns. He renders the stylized bodies with contours and gives little attention to the detailed modeling of their three-dimensional form.

Petkovski describes the Okean paintings as a symbolic visual ode to love and the joy of living, an exceptional instance in Yugoslav art at the time. In his view, the main impulse behind this wide panorama, whatever else might be suggested about its circumstances, was Martinoski’s wish to make monumental works that would be a modern counterpart to the old tradition of wall painting. He sees episodes in the painter’s earlier work as preparation for the subjects he addressed at the Okean.

I would qualify Petkovski’s assessment. There is too little evidence to establish the decorative scheme as a whole. It is clear that the pictures were made for a nightspot and that they had subjects suited to it. Some of the works, however, do not appear lascivious. By making the female nude the central motif of a cycle, Martinoski moved away from the everyday subjects of his other work. Scenes of nightlife occur in his paintings of the early 1930s, whether they show drinkers, café revelers, or portraits of sex workers. A comparison with the photos of the Okean frescoes makes the distinction clear. The paintings deal with human vice, sin, and weakness. The café frescoes show a mythical Arcadia, where the wine flows freely and the joy of living does not fade.

Finally, two poems by Nikola Martinoski appear in the anthology Yugoslav Interwar Bohemian Poetry. The second is titled The Okean Café.
