Born in Skopje in 1974, Hristina Ivanoska works across drawing, textiles, performance, installation, and public space. She and Yane Calovski co-founded Press to Exit Project Space in 2004.

Artist’s website

Selected Works

In Three Actions (2017), Ivanoska performs three scenes that Rosa Plaveva might have enacted in 1903. Plaveva was a Macedonian revolutionary who organized for women’s and workers’ rights. Ivanoska drew on documents and oral accounts to stage a series of possibilities. The performance belongs to Document Missing, which she began in 2014.

Ivanoska returned to Plaveva’s family in the 2022 performance Daughter, following the life of Nadezhda, who also faced political persecution.

Naming the Bridge: Rosa Plaveva and Nakie Bajram

When Skopje was to get a new bridge in 2004, Ivanoska proposed naming it after Rosa Plaveva and Nakie Bajram. The city did not adopt the proposal. She took it up again in 2021 through Women for Women, co-produced by Tiiiit! Inc. The name would have put both women into everyday speech each time someone gave directions across the city.

Easy Accumulation of Capital

To make Easy Accumulation of Capital in 2019, Ivanoska signed a contract with the Museum of Contemporary Art in Skopje to embroider under terms drawn from her research into Macedonia’s garment industry. The contract went on display beside the textile and mural, where visitors could read the conditions under which the work was made. The title refers to Rosa Luxemburg’s The Accumulation of Capital. A later version appeared in Art at Work in Ljubljana in 2022.

Freedom, Emancipation, Education, Labour!

Across fourteen meters of wall at the Museum of Yugoslavia in Belgrade, Cyrillic letters interlock in a typeface Ivanoska calls Archetype Open Form. She made the mural in 2024 in response to a sketch by Đorđe Andrejević Kun for a monument in Ivanjica.

What Cannot Be Closed Within Itself

For What Cannot Be Closed Within Itself, shown in Skopje in 2026, Ivanoska worked with dry pigment on a six-meter mural and layered cut paper. Both use the forms of Archetype Open Form. Words including scar, world, and body emerge from the cuts.
