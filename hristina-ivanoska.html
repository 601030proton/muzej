Focus 01

Contemporary art, examined.

Hristina Ivanoska

A name on a bridge.
A voice in a room.

For Hristina Ivanoska, researching women’s histories leads to actions in the present: a proposal to a city council, a script spoken before an audience, a garment waiting to be worn.

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

The nameThe voiceThe body

01 / Public space

A name
in public

A citizen can ask a city to name a bridge. In 2004, Hristina Ivanoska began a project that put that ordinary procedure to use. She proposed naming a new crossing in Skopje after Rosa Plaveva and Nakie Bajram, two women associated with the struggle for women’s emancipation. Research, meetings, photographs and a written request became the materials of Naming the Bridge.

Ivanoska’s early account describes discovering the naming procedure in a newspaper. The invitation to participate was there; recognition did not follow automatically. At the time she wrote that account, the city had not acted on her request. She published the proposal in the magazine Forum, giving it another route into public life.

A bridge name is a small piece of history repeated through use. It enters directions, addresses and conversations. Putting these women’s names there would ask the city to remember them during an ordinary journey. The project makes recognition a practical matter, involving a place, an administrative decision and the people who use it.

02 / Performance

The document
speaks

Plaveva returns in Document Missing, the research project Ivanoska began in 2014. Its sources include oral testimonies about women’s political activity in Macedonia, transcribed and preserved after the Second World War. Ivanoska turns this material into texts and performances, taking on the role of the person who must speak it.

The gaps in the record remain part of the work. The Academy of Fine Arts Vienna described The Interrogation, presented there in 2016, as an imaginative interpretation of a supposed interrogation of Plaveva, possibly in 1951. It was not presented as an authenticated transcript. Ivanoska works with the uncertain space between an account, its interpretation and the event it describes.

That distinction gives the performance its tension. A document can be consulted at a distance; a person speaking its words occupies the same room as an audience. Her breath, timing and physical presence become part of how the history reaches us. The artist is responsible for choices the archive cannot make on her behalf.

“What does it mean to be both a creator and a citizen today?”

Hristina Ivanoska, published artist statement, Secondary Archive, 2025

03 / Material

Text, voice,
and body

A magazine offered another way into this question. Working with historian Ivana Hadzievska and ethnologist and activist Jana Kocevska, Ivanoska researched Makedonka, the magazine of the Women’s Antifascist Front of Macedonia. Published from 1944 to 1952, it brought women’s emancipatory concerns into print. Her attention settled on its first issue, produced in November 1944.

The research prompted her to examine the work of making and editing such material. For the installation Document Missing: On Methodology, she identified three elements of her own practice: text, voice and body. They offer a useful way to follow what happens between research and performance. Words are selected and arranged; someone gives them a voice; a body makes that act visible.

The garment introduces the possibility of a wearer even when nobody is inside it. In the installation photograph, it stands before a window, its sleeves and folded fabric occupying a person’s space. Placed alongside performance, the unoccupied garment makes absence tangible. It asks us to look at what is waiting to be activated.

In Flesh into Blossom, the garment appears amid pink, orange and purple forms spreading across a gallery wall. The scale shifts between marks on cloth and a surrounding field of color. These photographs show how far Ivanoska’s materials can travel from the apparent order of a written document.

Her work gives history different conditions of encounter. On a bridge, a name can become familiar through repetition. In performance, a voice holds an audience’s attention for a particular duration. A garment makes room for a body, or makes its absence visible. Each form asks something different of the people who meet it.

The bridge proposal remains the clearest expression of the stakes. Research can recover a name; public recognition requires another action. Ivanoska takes that next step, bringing the history she has found to the institutions and audiences who can choose to acknowledge it.

About the artist

Hristina Ivanoska was born in Skopje in 1974. Her practice includes performance, drawing, textiles, installation and work in public space. With Yane Calovski, she co-founded Press to Exit Project Space in Skopje in 2004.
