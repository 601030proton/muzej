Born in Skopje in 1974, Ivanoska works across drawing, textiles, performance, installation and public space. With Yane Calovski, she co-founded Press to Exit Project Space in 2004.

Artist’s website

Making a history visible

In Hristina Ivanoska’s recent cut-paper drawings, the openings matter as much as the shapes. White, red, gray and violet forms overlap; a cut in one layer reveals another beneath it. Words written in her typography, Archetype Open Form, become structures to look through. The 2026 installation What Cannot Be Closed Within Itself brings seven such drawings together with a dry-pigment mural.

The changes of scale are part of the encounter. A drawing invites close attention to its cuts and edges. A mural surrounds that smaller object with a field of color. Her work asks language to occupy space, and asks the viewer to spend time with how it has been made.

A name in public

In Naming the Bridge, language has a direct civic task. Beginning in 2004, Ivanoska proposed naming a new crossing in Skopje after Rosa Plaveva and Nakie Bajram, two women associated with the struggle for women’s emancipation. She had learned that citizens could submit naming requests to the city council. Research, meetings, correspondence and photographs became the materials of the project.

Her early account records that the city had not acted on the request at that stage. She published her proposal in Forum, giving it another public life. A name on a bridge enters everyday directions and conversations. The project asks who receives that ordinary, repeated form of recognition.

A document performed

Plaveva returns in Document Missing, Ivanoska’s research project begun in 2014. Its sources include oral testimonies about women’s political activity in Macedonia, transcribed and preserved after the Second World War. The artist turns this material into scripts and performances. The historical account reaches an audience through her voice, timing and physical presence.

The work keeps uncertainty visible. The Interrogation, presented at the Academy of Fine Arts Vienna in 2016, was described as an imaginative interpretation of a supposed interrogation of Plaveva, possibly in 1951. It was not an authenticated transcript. The difference between what survives and what an artist constructs remains central to the encounter.

The work of making

Embroidery gives time and labor a material presence. In Easy Accumulation of Capital, Ivanoska connects needlework to the exploitation of women in Macedonia’s textile industry. The photographs show the project moving between fabric and the scale of a wall. The subject is carried through the work of making it.

Across these different forms, she gives research a public consequence. A proposal goes to a city council. A script is spoken before an audience. A word becomes a shape cut by hand. The history has to take a form that another person can encounter.
