Hristina
Ivanoska

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Letters become drawings, stitches, and public demands in the Macedonian artist’s work.

Pink paper curves around an opening. Red appears beneath it; another layer interrupts the shape. Across the installation, curves and sharp edges suggest parts of something that might be a letter, a plant, or a body. In Hristina Ivanoska’s What Cannot Be Closed Within Itself, shown at the Museum of Contemporary Art in Skopje in 2026, looking and reading keep getting in each other’s way.

Words are embedded in these forms: body, joyous, лузна, свет. The last two are Macedonian for scar and world. Ivanoska has written them in Archetype Open Form, a typography she uses to pull language into the physical business of making art. Here, that means cut paper and pigment applied directly to a wall. A word has an outline, an interior, something another shape can cover.

The work offers a useful introduction to an artist whose materials range from embroidery to municipal correspondence. Ivanoska is interested in who gets to speak and how their words survive. Following that interest takes her into archives, onto walls, and into performances in which she becomes the person delivering the text. It also leads to an awkward question for anyone looking at her art: how much do we expect to understand immediately?

An alphabet would usually help with that. Hers complicates it. For Skopje Pride Weekend in 2024, she made a drawing using her typography that became a festival poster, with graphic design by Igor Delov. The organizers explicitly connected the difficulty of reading it to the difficulty of accepting people who resist familiar categories. The lettering asked the reader to spend time with something that would not resolve on command.

That is a demanding job for a poster, a format generally expected to get its message across quickly. It is also an appealing proposition: the form of an invitation can ask something of the person receiving it. Before agreeing with a message, you have to work out how to read it.

In BLOSSOMS (2025), eight cutout drawings represent the eight letters of the title. Overlaid, they become the motif commissioned for an EVN holiday greeting card. A word that could be typed in a second becomes a set of separate objects, each contributing to an image that the word alone cannot supply. It is a small example of how thoroughly Ivanoska can reconsider a familiar piece of communication.

Elsewhere, language has a much more explicit public task. Her long-running bridge project proposed the names Rosa Plaveva and Nakie Bajram for a new crossing in Skopje. In an early account, Ivanoska describes learning that citizens could submit naming requests to the city council. She used that procedure to make a proposal honoring two women associated with the struggle for women’s emancipation, one Macedonian and one Turkish. Meetings, research, photographs, and written records became part of the work.

There is something wonderfully exact about the choice of a bridge. Its name enters directions, conversations, and daily journeys. The proposal makes historical recognition a practical question: whose name do people say when explaining how to get somewhere? It also places the artist in an unfamiliar position. She is a citizen filling out a request, asking an institution to act on the history she has researched.

Plaveva returns in Document Missing, Ivanoska’s research and performance project begun in 2014. Its sources include archived oral testimony about women’s political and emancipatory activity in Macedonia. One strand developed through research with historian Ivana Hadzievska and ethnologist and activist Jana Kocevska into Makedonka, the magazine of the Women’s Antifascist Front of Macedonia, published between 1944 and 1952. Ivanoska focused on its first issue, produced in November 1944.

Making a magazine means deciding what should be said, who should say it, and how it will reach another person. Those decisions connect a historical publication to her own practice with unusual directness. In the account accompanying Document Missing: On Methodology, the research leads her toward three working elements: text, voice, and body. A document can supply the words; a performance changes the circumstances in which we hear them.

The distinction between evidence and invention matters here. The Interrogation, presented in Vienna in 2016, was described by the Academy of Fine Arts as an imaginative interpretation of a supposed interrogation of Plaveva, possibly in 1951. The uncertainty belongs to the work. Ivanoska writes and performs within the gaps in the record, making interpretation visible rather than presenting a recovered transcript as complete.

Her choice of embroidery brings another kind of labor into view. Easy Accumulation of Capital, developed in mural and embroidered forms, addresses the exploitation of women in Macedonia’s textile industry. In her published artist statement, she connects needlework’s association with women’s labor to the economic conditions the project examines. The material carries part of the argument: the time spent making something belongs in our understanding of its value.

There is a risk in discussing such work entirely through its subject. Once the historical injustice has been identified, the object can become an illustration of a position we already accept. Ivanoska’s changes of material resist that easy reading. An embroidered text and a mural make different demands on attention. Scale, pace, and the relationship between a hand and a surface all matter.

At the Museum of Yugoslavia in Belgrade in 2024, her mural Freedom, Emancipation, Education, Labour! entered a direct conversation with Đorđe Andrejević Kun’s design for a memorial mosaic in Ivanjica. The four words were rendered in her own Cyrillic typography. Placed beside Kun’s historical work, they invited a contemporary examination of the commitments named in the title. What happens to those commitments when they become museum material? What would it take to treat them as demands again?

Her practice also extends to creating places where other people can work. In 2004, she and Yane Calovski established Press to Exit Project Space in Skopje, with programs for research, production, visiting curators, publications, and exchange. That work belongs alongside the performances and installations: it gives artistic inquiry a practical structure through which to circulate.

Read together, these projects make the tension in the recent cutouts more interesting. A proposal for a bridge name asks for explicit recognition. An overlapping letterform can withhold immediate recognition. Both choices have a place in Ivanoska’s work. A woman’s contribution to political history deserves to be known; that does not mean every person must become transparent to anyone who looks at them.

The pleasure of the work lies in having to adjust. You approach a drawing as an image and discover a word. You follow a historical reference and encounter an artist making decisions in the present. Ivanoska keeps changing the terms of attention, giving us something to do with the histories she brings into view.
