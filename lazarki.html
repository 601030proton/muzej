Words Ace Trajkovski

Lazarki

Through Lazarki, ritual, song, and dress travel door to door.

On Saturday, the girls go to a willow tree near a monastery. They select living branches and sing as they gather them, then carry the branches back towards the monastery in procession.

At the chapel entrance, each Lazarka attaches willow to the doorway. Villagers interviewed during the research could no longer explain the reason for the gesture. They knew that earlier generations had done it, and they continued to do so.

Within the monastery complex, the girls sing in structured formations and place willow near or before the icon of the patron saint. They then visit other religious sites, including monasteries dedicated to the Virgin Mary, St Michael the Archangel, St Spas and St Demeter, completing the circuit before sunset.

These actions belong to Lazarki, a group-based ritual practice associated with the calendar around Lazarus Saturday and Palm Sunday. In its documented forms, girls and young women move through villages singing blessings, performing prescribed movements and dances, wearing ceremonial clothing, carrying ritual objects and receiving gifts from the households they visit.

The objects vary by locality and period: baskets, willow branches, staffs, umbrellas and hats all appear in the record, as do weapons in older descriptions.

The common pattern does not amount to a standardised custom. Across Struga, Drimkol, Radoviš, Prespa, Dolni Polog, Kozjak, Kriva Palanka and elsewhere, the participants, dress, repertoire, movements, ritual days and internal roles differ.

The following day, the procession moves from religious sites into the settlement. The girls divide into smaller groups, visit households and sing songs appropriate to those receiving them. Gifts, including money, are collected and later combined with ritual bread, or pogača, before being redistributed among the performers.

The willow in Injevo also resists a single explanation. In Slavic Orthodox practice, willow substitutes for palm on Palm Sunday because it is locally available and buds early in spring. Macedonian ethnographic interpretations have also associated its gathering with vegetation, fertility and seasonal regeneration. These are distinct contexts, not interchangeable explanations of the chapel-door gesture.

What is documented is its place in the spring cycle: willow is gathered and carried, brought into religious and domestic spaces, and associated with Orthodox Palm Sunday observance. There is no evidence that the particular Injevo gesture is an unchanged survival of a named pre-Christian Macedonian religion, or that its earlier meaning can now be recovered. The participants themselves could not explain it.

The household and its visitors

Preparations traditionally begin several days before the procession. Historical descriptions most often record groups of approximately four to eight girls, although the age and composition of groups vary by locality and period. Girls learned songs from older women, prepared baskets and other objects and wore clothing that was exceptionally clean and festive. A commonly reported historical age range is approximately 12–17, but younger children and older unmarried women also appear in regional accounts.

The repertoire was not a single generic song repeated at every house. Songs could be selected according to the person or circumstances being addressed: an unmarried young man, bride, mother, child, elderly person, absent household member or prosperous house, as well as crops, livestock and other aspects of village life. Households could request particular songs for individual members.

The degree of attention given to individual household members is already visible in the Miladinov brothers’ 1861 collection. Lazar songs from the Struga area are categorised by addressee: a small child, an unmarried man, a bride, a woman with sons, a childless woman whose husband is away, a wealthy man or an old man.

The accompanying description records girls gathering by neighbourhood, selecting one girl as a ceremonial nevesta, and singing differently according to the age and status of the person encountered. The household therefore does not receive an anonymous blessing. Its members are individually recognized through a repertoire adapted to their social position.

The songs are followed by an exchange. Historical sources record gifts of food, eggs, cloth, clothing and money; contemporary groups may collect money as well as eggs or food. Traditionally the gifts were divided among the performers, and in Injevo the money is still pooled and redistributed.

The exchange is therefore better understood as ritual reciprocity than as payment for entertainment: the household receives blessing and social recognition, while the performers receive material acknowledgment. The ritual also has limits. Historical accounts describe Lazarki avoiding houses where a recent death had occurred. In contemporary Injevo, mourning still affects whether a household receives the ritual, although the length of mourning is now determined by the family.

The Lazarki arrive at a household, where a welcoming song marks entry. The household identifies who should be addressed, and dance may take place in the courtyard. Gifts are received before a farewell song marks departure and the group continues to the next house. This repeated exchange temporarily converts private domestic spaces into ceremonial spaces. In Injevo, this domestic network is preceded by a network of monasteries and religious sites.

Song, movement and social roles

The songs do more than describe a blessing: within the ritual, singing is itself the act of blessing. Macedonian ritual dance-songs have been described as collective forms in which melody, rhythm and choreographic movement operate together. Lazar songs are strongly associated with antiphonal or responsorial performance.

Reported metres include 2/4 and 7/8. Depending on locality, one group may begin and another repeat, or singers may alternate in pairs or smaller groups. Veličkovska’s study of northeastern traditions distinguishes functional categories including:

na odenje / na šetanje
Songs performed while moving
na stojanje
Songs performed while standing, often with the refrain Lazare
na vrtenje / na igranje
Songs directly associated with turning and dancing

The distinction is functional: the repertoire changes according to whether the performers are walking, standing or dancing.

Participation is generally associated with unmarried girls, but the group has its own hierarchy. Its leader may be called tančarka, nevesta/nesta, matica, prednjica, navrhnica or zaveljvač, depending on locality. Often the oldest or most accomplished performer, she may greet a household ceremonially or speak for the group.

Other local systems distinguish prednjici or čelnici from srednjici and other positions. Vevčani records the terms buljubaša, zavelvač and concluding singer. Some traditions also assign explicitly gendered roles, including Maški Lazar and Ženski Lazar—Male and Female Lazar.

Masculine attributes may include a staff, hat, umbrella, sword or weapon. Historical descriptions from Kozjak record a girl in male clothing carrying a yatagan or knife at the front of the procession. The evidence does not establish a single explanation for the origin of these roles.

The movement vocabulary is similarly varied: walking, braided steps, stamping, turns, jumps, interlacing or serpentine movement and alternating lines. In the Struga region, where song and dance are particularly well documented, Mihajlo Dimovski’s 1973 study records dance-songs including:

“Brala moma, Lazare, zelenika”
“Kito Mito nevesto”
“Vijur Vijur Gajdanot”

In Tašmaruništa, Petar Avramovski records a specific relationship between song, kinship and dance: after “Kito Mito Nevesto,” the girls begin an oro, and the person who leads the dance should be a girl related to the household receiving the blessing.

The same tradition could carry a life-stage meaning. In the Struga area, a girl approaching marriage could take the final position in the chain, in what was understood as her last performance of that particular ritual dance as an unmarried girl. The movement described for Brala moma zelenika, by contrast, is a calm open-circle dance moving to the right, without leaps.

Dress and the passage to adulthood

Lazarki clothing is closely associated with bridal dress and adult female status. Historical participants could wear garments or ornaments normally associated with brides, sometimes for the first time. Petkovski interprets this as an initiatory dimension: the girl temporarily assumes the visual vocabulary of the marriageable or married woman. An older maxim cited in the literature held that it was shameful for a Lazarka to sing in old clothing. Newness, cleanliness and completeness were important, although the specific distinction from everyday clothing varied by locality.

In the Struga area, documented and reconstructed examples include white shirts, woven aprons, waistcoats or bodices, headcloths, necklaces and substantial displays of coins or metal ornaments. Baskets may be decorated with flowers or spring greenery. One Struga-region description lists a klašenik, shirt, belt, woven apron or bovča, headscarf and jewellery including head ornaments, earrings and chains bearing coins. Aprons could be vertically striped and decorated with coloured pompoms, metallic sequins and lace. In northeastern areas, some historical costumes approached bridal dress while being distinguished by particular headwear and props.

In Sredorek, Žegligovo and Slavište, descriptions include the knitted kapa šehirlika, while umbrellas were carried specifically by the first two girls. In Prespa, the leading Lazarka could carry a willow staff and use it to knock on household doors.

Injevo offers a more recent, closely documented example of the clothing worn by participants in 2024:

White inner garments embroidered at the collar, sleeves and hem
A dark woven outer apron with geometric, floral and animal motifs
An embroidered or appliquéd narrow vest
Thick socks
Opanci
Braided hair
A red rose above the right ear
Colourful necklaces

One participant wore an elaborate beaded necklace resembling a snake. Informants reported that family matriarchs had once commissioned and passed down costumes, and that embroidery had previously communicated marital status more strictly than it does today. Costume consequently operates not simply as decoration but as part of the ritual’s system of status, age and gender.

The association between dress and life stage has led scholars to describe Lazarki in terms of initiation, particularly where girls wear bridal attributes or perform a ritual dance for the final time before marriage. The evidence supports a connection to transition, though not a rigid three-stage initiation model applicable to every region.

What is clear is that the ritual publicly distinguishes the participating girls from childhood. They move independently through village space, address household heads, handle gifts and money, perform inherited repertoire and wear garments associated with adult female status. Courtship is also present in the song corpus. Young men, girls, flowers, bridal imagery and hoped-for marriages recur throughout the repertoire. The girls are publicly visible, some wear bridal attributes, and particular songs identify unmarried men and women. Yet these elements operate within a sanctioned religious-seasonal procession rather than a straightforward courtship event.

A ritual with regional forms

The differences become clearer when the practice is followed from village to village. Struga has a particularly substantial record, from nineteenth-century collections through twentieth-century ethnography to contemporary reporting, yet practices differ even within Struško and Debarski Drimkol.

Some are associated with Lazarus Saturday; in Struga, related performances can extend into Palm Sunday. Some villages preserve separate male and female forms. In Ložani, contemporary documentation describes Na konak, in which Lazarki spend the night before the feast in a newly built house. If a younger participant falls asleep, older girls may mischievously sew part of her clothing to the bedding.

In Vevčani, the women’s Lazarus custom disappeared around the 1950s and was revived in 2016 by KUD Drimkol. Current participants gather at St Nicholas, sing the Lazarus troparion, decorate baskets, perform a Lazarus oro in the centre and divide into groups visiting upper and lower neighbourhoods. Vevčani also preserves or has revived a Maška Lazara tradition involving boys. In Struga proper, modern summaries describe connected activities across three days:

Friday
Announcing the feast
Saturday
Associated with “Daj mi para Lazare”
Palm Sunday
Associated with “Vijur, vijur Gajdano”

Veličkovska’s territorial study finds a strong integration of ritual singing and dancing across the Ohrid–Struga lowland villages. The places recorded include Jankoec, Leskoec, Velgošti, Mešeišta, Trebeništa, Kosel, Livoišta, Volino, Moroišta, Ložani, Draslajca, Labuništa, Misleševo, Radožda, Vraništa, Vevčani and Modrič.

In the northeast, the contrasts are different. Accounts from Kozjak and Kriva Palanka describe more differentiated roles, masculine disguise, crossing groups, umbrellas and weapons, alongside songs organised by whether performers walk, stand or turn and dance.

In Radoviš, Vijena Loza Vijena was performed three times before a household; informants also remembered an oro following the songs, accompanied by gajda and tapan. Older Prespa accounts describe a leading Lazarka carrying a willow staff, while Kozjak descriptions record masculine dress and weapons. Documentation from Kriva Palanka / Slavište includes Male and Female Lazar, umbrellas and distinct song types for walking, standing and turning.

In Dolni Polog, the available contemporary material comes from a filmed reconstruction rather than uninterrupted ritual practice. The project combined literature and field research with costume selection, scripting, rehearsal and filming.

What the historical record can tell us

The earliest substantial written evidence discussed here comes from the Miladinov brothers’ 1861 collection of Lazar songs from the Struga area. Published alongside wedding, harvest and other calendrical songs, the material records a practice already organised around neighbourhood groups and differentiated household addresses.

The accompanying description includes house-to-house movement, the ceremonial nevesta, decorative staffs and songs selected according to the age and status of their recipients. It documents a nineteenth-century practice; it does not establish its antiquity.

No surviving documents demonstrate a Lazarki procession in Macedonia before Christianisation, and the 1861 material cannot prove direct continuity from a pre-Christian ritual. Lazarki is observed within the Christian calendar: Lazarus Saturday commemorates Christ’s raising of Lazarus of Bethany in John 11 and precedes Palm Sunday. In Orthodox liturgical practice, the feasts form a connected passage into Holy Week. Their relation to the procession, however, is not fully explained by the biblical narrative. Some popular accounts associate the girls’ house-to-house movement with Martha and Mary, as though they were spreading news of Lazarus’s resurrection.

Petkovski discusses this as a folk explanation. John 11 does not describe Martha and Mary undertaking such a procession, so the association is better understood as a later Christian explanation of a ritual form, not evidence that the Gospel prescribed it.

A number of Lazarki elements have been interpreted by comparative folklorists as archaic or agrarian:

Spring vegetation
Blessings for fields and livestock
Ritual circling
Jumping
Gendered disguise
Fertility wishes
Processional movement

Veličkovska discusses interpretations of turning, stamping and jumping in relation to wind, crop growth and fertility. Such similarities may inform comparative interpretation, but they do not demonstrate historical survival. Petkovski likewise notes the absence of documents establishing a Lazarki procession in Macedonia before Christianisation. Some ritual elements are not obviously derived from the Gospel narrative, yet their earlier history remains uncertain.

Lazarki belongs to a Christian calendrical framework while incorporating ritual forms—vegetation, household blessing, courtship, processional movement and fertility-oriented song—that scholars associate with older Balkan and South Slavic spring traditions. Direct continuity from a specific pre-Christian cult has not been historically demonstrated.

Continuity, revival and reconstruction

The distinction between practice and documentation matters equally in the present. In Injevo, direct fieldwork in 2024 records an active circuit of monasteries and households. Around Struga and Drimkol, village practices continue alongside teaching and preparation by churches and cultural organisations. Vevčani’s women’s tradition was revived after an interruption, while Dolni Polog has been the subject of a filmed reconstruction. Photographic exhibitions have also presented Lazarki as intangible heritage and visual culture.

These are not equivalent forms of continuity. A community revival, a filmed reconstruction and a stage choreography differ from a household ritual in their setting, participants and purposes. By the twentieth century, Lazarki material had also entered professional folklore institutions, including Tanec and Orce Nikolov.

Once the material moves to the stage, the structure changes: the house disappears, the recipient of the blessing disappears, neighbourhood movement disappears, songs are selected for performance rather than household circumstance, and dance becomes choreography. The staged form is not necessarily false; it is a different cultural and artistic form from the ritual that generated it.

The distinction also raises the question of transmission. Traditionally, songs were learned from mothers and grandmothers. Participants in contemporary Injevo continue to learn through practice: one interviewed in 2024 had learned the repertoire only two months before the fieldwork, while others had participated for several years. This way of transmitting knowledge can sustain a ritual without written codification, but it can also leave individual gestures in use after their explanations have faded.

An archive maintained through women’s bodies

One useful art-historical interpretation is to read Lazarki as an archive maintained through women’s bodies. Songs passed principally through women, while costumes were made, preserved and inherited within domestic networks. Girls learned formations and movements from older participants, appeared publicly in bridal attributes and occupied ranked positions within their groups. In some traditions, girls even performed masculine ceremonial roles.

Much of this knowledge was never primarily stored in manuscripts or institutions. It existed in voice, textiles, gesture, posture, memory and repetition.

RESEARCH STATUS

The Lazarki record is unusually rich but uneven.

The Struga and Struško material has a particularly long documentary history, beginning with the Miladinov brothers in the nineteenth century and continuing through twentieth-century ethnographic and ethnomusicological research.

Injevo provides unusually strong contemporary evidence through direct fieldwork conducted in 2024 and published in 2025.

Northeastern Macedonian traditions are also supported by substantial ethnomusicological documentation.

Other areas remain less thoroughly documented.

The distinction between documented practice, historical description, community revival, reconstruction, staged performance and interpretation is therefore essential to any account of Lazarki.

The available evidence supports a picture of a highly varied ritual system in which song, movement, dress, household relationships, religious observance and seasonal practice are closely connected.

It does not support reducing that system to a single origin story.
