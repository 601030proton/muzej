The Nation on Their Backs

Five Macedonian shirts, and what they reveal about design, memory, and the limits of reinvention.

The shirt they would not wear

In May 2021, weeks before Macedonia’s first European Championship, the football federation unveiled a burgundy shirt with an embossed geometric lynx across the front. Supporters wanted the old one back. Within days, the federation announced that the team would keep its red-and-yellow sun kit for the tournament.

The objections were unusually specific. The red was too dark; the familiar yellow had receded; the lynx had replaced the sun. A petition called for the previous shirt to be retained. Footballer Natasha Andonova publicly defended red and yellow as colors with deep personal meaning. The dispute concerned the appearance of a garment, but it came at a moment when that garment would be seen by the audience for its first major tournament.

It is tempting to treat the episode as proof that national shirts must never change. The earlier designs suggest otherwise. Macedonia had played in quite different arrangements of red and yellow before the sun spread across the torso. The contrast between those shirts helps explain how far the burgundy design departed from what supporters knew.

Red and yellow

The 1994 Gems home shirt offers a quiet starting point. A yellow polo collar and a buttoned placket sit above a red body with a subtle jacquard texture. Most of the shirt’s interest is in details that reward a close look: the surface of the fabric, the fastening, the meeting of collar and chest. From a distance, the red dominates.

Its restraint is appealing. The collar gives the shirt a recognizable shape without competing with the body, and the textured fabric keeps the broad red surface from looking flat. There is no need to read every detail as a national statement. Some of its character comes from the ordinary construction of a football shirt.

Across the chest

The 1998 Puma home shirt makes a more assertive use of the same colors. A broad yellow band, edged in white, crosses the chest and sleeves. It breaks at the center to leave space for the Puma logo. Yellow cuffs and a red collar with yellow-and-white trim repeat the contrast. If the Gems shirt used color as accent, the Puma shirt used it as structure.

The band is the first thing the eye finds. It divides the torso and remains visible when the smaller details disappear. Beside the Gems shirt, it shows how much can change without abandoning the palette: one concentrates yellow at the neckline; the other carries it across the body.

A sun worth keeping

JAKO’s 2016 shirt changed the relationship between the badge and the rest of the garment. Rays spread outward from the federation crest, drawing the surrounding fabric into the design. The badge no longer sits alone on a largely plain chest; it becomes the center of a much larger composition.

The effect is more expansive than Puma’s horizontal band. The rays lead the eye in several directions, giving the front of the shirt an uneven, energetic rhythm. Red and yellow remain recognizable, but the sun now supplies the shape.

By November 2020, this design also had a particular achievement attached to it. Macedonia had qualified for its first European Championship. In its announcement celebrating qualification, JAKO explicitly connected the sun shirt with the team’s journey to the tournament. The manufacturer also promised a new outfit for the following summer.

That history complicates the rejection of the lynx shirt. Its replacement would have displaced a garment already associated with qualification. The burgundy color and tonal animal graphic offered a much quieter contrast than the old rays. Whatever the merits of the lynx as a subject, the new shirt looked markedly different at precisely the moment supporters were preparing to celebrate the team.

Contemporary reporting on the backlash documents objections to both the colors and the graphic. It does not establish that supporters would reject a lynx under all circumstances, or that every future shirt needed the same sun. The federation had changed several familiar features at once, just before the team’s tournament debut.

Moving the sun

The sun shirt remained in use until a new JAKO design arrived in 2023. The rejected 2021 kit was therefore an interruption in the design process, rather than a two-year departure on the pitch.

The 2023 home shirt retained the sun but altered its position and emphasis. The rays were more subdued and centered around the player’s number rather than the federation crest. In the shirt pictured here, short yellow and orange strokes build the rays around an open red center. Yellow cuffs give the sleeves a firmer edge.

The familiar subject allowed room for a different treatment. Solid rays had given way to clusters of short lines. Where the 2016 rays drew attention toward the badge, the newer composition gathered around the number at the center of the chest. On the unnumbered shirt, that center is empty: a space waiting for the player.
