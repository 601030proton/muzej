Vranec

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Three bottles and a dinner-table guide to Macedonia’s familiar red.

If you grew up with T’ga za jug on the table, you might expect Vranec to taste a little sweet. Tikveš calls that wine semi-dry. Kamnik’s 2018 Grand Reserva is dry and clocks in at 16 percent alcohol. Then there’s Barovo Red, which blends Vranec with Kratošija. Three bottles from Macedonia, and the name of the grape gets you only so far.

Say you’re serving roast lamb with peppers. Are the peppers sweet from the oven, or hot enough that you reach for water? With the hotter plate, a little sweetness in the wine can be welcome. With the lamb and sweet peppers, I’d try the Barovo. If I opened the Kamnik, I’d pour a small glass and see how it changed over dinner.

The vineyard

Tikveš is both a wine district and the name of the winery in Kavadarci that makes T’ga za jug and Barovo Red. Barovo is the more specific name on that second label. The winery’s vineyards there reach about 700 meters in the mountains around Kožuf. According to Tikveš, the swing between warm days and cooler nights lets the grapes ripen more slowly and retain acidity. That acidity is part of what can make a ripe red refreshing with a rich bite of lamb.

There’s another detail worth knowing about Barovo Red. The Kratošija is part of the wine you’re tasting. Tikveš takes a similar approach at Bela Voda, where it blends Vranec with Plavec. If you like one of those wines, remember the name on the bottle. Buying another Vranec might take you somewhere else entirely.

Vranec has roots in Montenegro, where it’s known as Vranac. Writing for the Wine & Spirit Education Trust, Tikveš educator Zvonko Herceg traces the grape’s arrival in Macedonia to experimental plantings near Skopje after World War II. It yielded generously, which helped it catch on during the Yugoslav years. Today you can find it in wines made with particular sites in mind, like Barovo, as well as in bottles meant for everyday meals.

The cellar

Take a sip and notice how your mouth feels after you swallow. If your gums feel dry, as they might after a sip of strong black tea, you’re feeling tannin. Some of it comes from the skins and seeds, and how long the wine spends in contact with them helps shape that grip. Try another sip after a bite of lamb. You may find the same wine easier to drink with food.

Kamnik let some of the grapes for its 2018 Grand Reserva dry on the vine, then aged the wine for two years in new French and American oak. Drying concentrates the grapes, and new oak can bring aromas of toast or spice. Those details tell you more about the bottle than its dark color does. They also give you a few things to pay attention to as the wine opens up.

At the table

Kamnik recommends decanting that Grand Reserva and serving it at 59–64°F (15–18°C). If the bottle’s been sitting in a warm kitchen, give it a short spell in the fridge. Pour a little at first. You can see how it tastes cool and then again as it warms in the glass.

If you have two bottles and people to share them with, pour the wines side by side with the same meal. Try each on its own, then with a bite of lamb or a pepper. See which glass you reach for next. If you want to buy that bottle again, take a picture of the label. The producer and vintage will be there when you need them.
