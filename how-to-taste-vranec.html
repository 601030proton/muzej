How to Taste Vranec

Take a sip of Vranec. After you swallow, run your tongue along your upper gums. Can you still taste the fruit when you begin to feel that slight drag? Two Macedonian bottles give you a way to taste the difference.

The first glass

Begin with Tikveš Classic Vranec. Tikveš is the name of both the wine district and the winery that makes this bottle. Vranec is the grape. Leave the back label unread for a moment. Once someone suggests black cherries, it becomes remarkably easy to smell them.

Pour a little, leaving room to swirl. Smell the wine once while it is still, then again after a turn of the glass. If something reminds you of plum, stay with that thought. Is it the tart smell of a plum cut open, or the richer smell of plums cooking down for jam? Both descriptions start with the same fruit and take you somewhere quite different. Use whichever comes closest to what you smell, even if all you can say today is “ripe.”

Now read the winery’s notes. Tikveš describes black cherry and damson, a small plum, alongside pronounced tannins and acidity. The last two are particularly easy to explore together. Take another sip and let it reach your gums. Tannin produces the friction you can also feel after drinking strong black tea. Acidity gets the saliva flowing. Your gums can feel dry at the very moment your mouth starts to water.

Pause before the next sip. Which impression lasts longest? Perhaps the plum comes back as you breathe out. Perhaps the fruit fades while the grip remains. Either observation tells you more about this particular glass than its deep color could.

A second pour

Pour Filovski Vranec into a second glass and keep a little Tikveš in the first. Both producers suggest serving their wines at 16–18°C (61–64°F). Use similar glasses and pour roughly equal amounts. Smell them in turn. It is much easier to catch a difference when you can go back to the first wine while the second is fresh in your mind.

Filovski ages its Vranec with American oak for more than six months. The winery describes a toasted aroma alongside dark fruit. Try picking out the toast first, then return to the other glass. Which has the clearer fruit aroma? Which leaves a longer impression of fruit after you swallow? The answers may point to different bottles. Oak is one choice the grower has made, and these wines come from different producers. A difference you can taste is worth recording even when you cannot say exactly what caused it.

Filovski calls its wine dry. If the fruit smells sweet to you, hold that thought as you taste. The smell of blackberry preserves can suggest sugar before the wine even touches your tongue. Taste again and ask whether you find sweetness on the tongue as well. This is also where the word “dry” can trip people up. On the label it refers to the wine’s sugar level. In a remark about how your gums feel, it describes the touch of tannin.

At the table

Save some of each wine for dinner. Both wineries suggest grilled meat. Try a bite of kebapče, then return to the glass where the tannin felt most pronounced. Does it catch at your gums quite as much now? Taste the other wine with the same food. You may find that the bottle you preferred on its own has changed places with the one you want alongside the meal.

Write down the producer and the vintage from the label, then one thing you would remember a week later. “I liked the plum in the first glass, but went back to the second with the kebapčinja” is a perfectly useful tasting note. The next time somebody pours you a Vranec, you have a particular wine, and a particular dinner, to compare it with.
