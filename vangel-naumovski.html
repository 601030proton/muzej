Vangel Naumovski: Forms of Life

Painting, drawing, and architecture in the world of Vangel Naumovski.

Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Two figures occupy the center of one of Vangel Naumovski’s paintings. Their legs and torsos are easy to pick out, but higher up the bodies give way to broad red, yellow, and blue shapes. A pale line crosses both at the waist, then loops back across the lower half of the painting.

That line lets the eye move between the figures and the surrounding flowers without stopping at the edge of either body. Naumovski gives the flesh volume through soft shading and leaves some of the brightest surrounding shapes almost flat. The space between those two ways of painting is where the picture gets its tension.

Born in 1924 in Skrebatno, near Ohrid, Naumovski worked at several trades before establishing himself as an artist. The National Gallery’s retrospective catalog records his enrollment at art school in Skopje in 1945 and his expulsion two years later. He subsequently worked as a draftsman in a woodcarving atelier and, from 1963, at Ohrid’s construction and design company Trudbenik. He became an independent painter in 1969.

That history gives the familiar description “self-taught” some necessary detail. Drawing was part of his working life. Painting developed alongside practical employment, and his interests eventually extended into architectural models. The route was uneven, but the work itself shows sustained attention to the behavior of a line.

His early reception placed him within naïve art. A 1963 exhibition at Zagreb’s Gallery of Primitive Art helped establish that association, and the category remains part of his exhibition history. It tells us something about the institutions through which he became known. By itself, however, it leaves much of the mature painting unexplained.

In the yellow and red composition, the largest yellow shape fills most of the picture. Small flowers, black seed heads, and white stems cross it. A red sphere at the right has the shading of a solid object, though the surface beneath it curves away like a leaf. There is no clear horizon to tell us how far back any of this extends.

Compare that with the painting of the two figures. Here, a body supplies a familiar scale. The flowers and loops unsettle it: a pale curve can cross a waist, reach into the surrounding color, and return to the body without changing its weight. The effect depends on the precision of the line and the soft modeling of the flesh.

An earlier painting, Youthful Landscape (1960), offers a point of comparison in the National Gallery catalog. There, gaps between slender trunks lead into a woodland. The leaves already have the sharp, individual accents that give the later compositions their rhythm. As his work developed, Naumovski found ways to carry that rhythm through a picture without relying on the depth of a landscape.

The blue composition is quieter. Green and violet shapes collect near the bottom, leaving a large stretch of blue above them. A pale loop rises into that space and narrows to a thread. Where the lines cross, one curve can seem to pass behind another, even though there is little else to establish depth.

The same attention to line is visible in the catalog’s reproduction of Universe (1979), an ink drawing in which a male profile sits among pale curves and small white ovals. The face gives the eye a fixed point. The lines running beneath it carry the movement out toward the margin.

Lake Ohrid offered a particular environment for this attention to living forms. Writing in Porta 3 in 2024, architect Ivana Grupče reported that Naumovski had drawn endemic organisms for Ohrid’s Hydrobiological Institute. Her account reproduces a sheet of snail studies and describes work made through observation under a microscope.

That account gives the biological imagery a concrete connection to his working life. It does not make every painted curve an illustration of a particular organism. A shell, an organ, and a petal can suggest different readings of the same form, and the pictures leave room for those readings to coexist.

Naumovski also pursued these interests in three dimensions. Grupče’s account of the 2024 centenary exhibition records nine architectural models, including a proposal for a lakeside villa and a model associated with the 1967 opera and ballet competition in Skopje. Photographs document a terracotta design for Hotel Eros. These were proposals through which he tested curved forms as volumes.

The distinction matters. On a canvas, a pale ribbon can hover without support. A building has to accommodate entrances, rooms, circulation, and structure. His models raise the question of how much of the paintings’ freedom could survive those demands. Their interest lies in the attempt to give his visual language another set of conditions.

His Ohrid house and studio offer a more tangible encounter with that ambition. In the studio photograph, small stones cover a rounded façade, interrupted by a broad opening. The rough, closely packed surface gives the building a texture quite different from the smooth forms in his paintings. Through the glass, the artist and his work occupy the space together.

Grupče describes a garden with sculpture and water, and an entrance whose curving stonework continues into the boundary wall. Her account draws on a visit to the property and on recollections from Naumovski’s son, Rubens. The house becomes useful evidence of how his interests met in everyday surroundings.

Later exhibitions have continued to change the context in which the paintings are seen. The National Gallery’s 2016–2017 retrospective brought together about one hundred works. In 2025–2026, Weaving Worlds: Collections in Conversation in Ljubljana included Naumovski within a broader examination of art’s relationship with nature. The exhibition connected works from Skopje’s Museum of Contemporary Art and Ljubljana’s Moderna galerija.

In the photograph of the studio, the broad window interrupts a wall of tightly packed stones. Through that opening, you can see Naumovski at his easel. The building gives his long interest in curves a practical expression: the rounded wall frames a place to work.
