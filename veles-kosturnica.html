Words Aleksandar Trajkovski

Veles Kosturnica

A walk through the 1979 memorial in Veles, from the hillside stair to Petar Mazev’s mosaics.

Above Veles, two white concrete shells curve toward each other and stop. The gap between them opens onto a court. Beyond it, the memorial holds a burial chamber, a bronze tree, and a history laid out in stone across the walls.1, 2

01

The approach

From the foot of the hill, the stair is the clearest line in the complex. It climbs from a paved terrace beside a sunken circular amphitheater. The white roof enters the view well before the entrance does. Near the top, the gap between the two concrete halves marks the way into the court.2, 3

The memorial opened on October 11, 1979, on the anniversary of the 1941 uprising in Macedonia. Contributions from residents of Veles helped finance it. The site had to accommodate a burial chamber and public gatherings. Those uses explain why so much of the project lies outside the ossuary itself.1, 3, 4

02

From paper to concrete

Drawings from МАРХ.

Architect Savo Subotin and sculptor Ljubomir Denković, both from Novi Sad, won the design competition. Petar Mazev made the mosaics for the interior. The Municipality of Veles describes the roof as a poppy opening into flower. МАРХ also records a reading of the silhouette as a cracked helmet. From the stair, the split is visible all the way down to the entrance.1, 2, 3

The ground-floor plan shows how much of the circle belongs to the open air. Its curved perimeter embraces the court on one side and encloses the memorial rooms on the other. In section, the two concrete shells rise separately. The break lets daylight into the court and makes the division legible from the stair.2

03

The court and the crypt

At the top of the stair, the horizon drops from view. The walls of the court bring the eye back to the ribbed concrete, the plaques, and the glazed doorway into the interior. Above them, a narrow opening between the shells keeps the sky in view.2, 3

The word kosturnica means ossuary. The crypt below holds the remains of 87 people associated with the local Partisan resistance. Among them is Kočo Racin, whose collection Beli mugri (White Dawns) was published in 1939. The room above the burial chamber gives the visitor a place to pause before turning to Mazev’s account of the history that brought them there.2, 3, 7

04

Painting with stone

In Kruševo, Mazev made the ceramic walls of Makedonium’s open-air amphitheater. In Veles, he worked across roughly 220 square meters of interior wall. Looking back on the commission, he said, “I simply painted with pieces of stone.” Natural stone and Venetian mosaic give the surface the shifts of color and texture that his figures depend on.1, 5, 6

Mazev traces a history from Ottoman rule and the Ilinden Uprising through the Partisan struggle and into postwar reconstruction. In the photographed detail, blue and black outlines pull the bodies across the wall. Elsewhere, a woman raises a clenched fist to mark victory. In the final scenes, children and birds carry the story into peacetime. The long wall makes this a sequence the visitor walks alongside.3, 4, 6

05

Veles during World War II

German aircraft bombed Veles on April 6, 1941, as Axis forces invaded Yugoslavia. The city came under Bulgarian occupation after the Yugoslav army capitulated. By the following spring, students and women’s groups were protesting in Veles, and Partisan detachments were organizing in the surrounding area.3

On November 9, 1944, Partisan forces including the Eighth Veles National Liberation Brigade liberated the city. Crowds lined the street as the Partisans marched through the center. Veles still marks November 9 as the city’s holiday.3, 8

The remains of fallen fighters were first buried at the Church of St. Panteleimon in Veles. In 1979, they were moved to the new ossuary. The memorial opened that October, thirty-five years after the fighting recorded in these photographs.3, 4
