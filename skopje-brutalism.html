A Spatial Field Guide to Concrete Skopje

Skopje’s post-earthquake architecture is often discussed through its scale and concrete. The more revealing story is how these buildings organize movement, repetition, public life, and civic identity.

Telecommunications Centre and Central Post Office

Janko KonstantinovTelecommunications complex designed from 1968; later phases completed in the 1970s and early 1980s

The complex began as an urban system, not a single sculptural object. Early proposals by Konstantinov’s team separated the program into three parts—the telecommunications center, public counter hall, and administrative building—set on a common platform. The arrangement echoed an important idea in postwar architecture: a large institution could be assembled from distinct functional units and connected through a shared ground plane. Research on the project identifies Japanese Metabolism, including Kenzo Tange’s urban projects, as a reference for its early development.

That matters when reading the building today. Rather than treating the complex as one dramatic mass, look for the relationship between its different functions and the common base that ties them together. The built scheme evolved from the early proposals, so the drawings are evidence of the design process, not a literal blueprint of everything that stands there now.

The Counter Hall is the most recognizable part: its ribbed concrete structure gives the interior a strong radial order. A fire in 2013 damaged the hall and destroyed its distinctive roof structure, making the building a difficult case study in how to conserve modern architecture when a defining part of its material identity has been lost.

Why it mattersThe complex makes the tension between architecture as a unified image and architecture as a set of working systems unusually legible.

Further readingSeasoned Modernism: Prudent Perspectives on the Architecture of Skopje for the early proposals and their relationship to Metabolism; Europa Nostra and the Institute of European Investment and Conservation for the Counter Hall’s conservation history.

City Trade Centre (GTC)

Živko Popovski and a multidisciplinary team1969–73Kej 13 Noemvri

GTC’s central idea is not the shopping mall as an enclosed destination, but retail woven into the routes of the city.

The competition brief, developed after the 1963 earthquake, called for commercial premises, restaurants, and services in a central site where pedestrian and vehicular movement intersected. Popovski’s project, selected from a Yugoslav competition in 1967 and developed with a large local team, became a five-level complex with multiple entrances, terraces, ramps, and internal passages. The design incorporates five existing residential towers along the riverfront rather than clearing the site for a single, self-contained object.

That decision is the key to understanding GTC. Its circulation is not simply a way to reach shops; it is the public space the building contributes to the center. Movement crosses levels and cuts through the complex, allowing the building to function as a shortcut, meeting place, and commercial address at once. The distinction matters: a conventional mall draws people inside, while GTC makes its routes part of the city’s pedestrian network.

The controversy over the proposed neoclassical cladding under the Skopje 2014 program exposed the consequences of this approach. The proposal was not merely a change of facade. It threatened the legibility of a building whose identity depends on open circulation, concrete terraces, and its relationship with surrounding streets. The cladding plan was abandoned.

On siteCross the building rather than viewing it from one side. Follow the ramps and passages, and notice where the interior becomes an extension of the street.

Further readingThe original competition material reproduced by MARH is particularly useful for understanding the design process and the alternatives considered.

City Archive of Skopje

Georgi Konstantinovski1966–68Corner of Partizanski Odredi Boulevard and Moskovska Street

The City Archive is a useful place to study how an institutional building can be composed as a group of related volumes rather than a single block.

Konstantinovski’s surviving drawings at the Museum of Modern Art include a ground-floor plan and an elevation, both dated 1966–68 and cataloged at 1:200. Their pairing offers a direct way to compare the building’s spatial arrangement with its exterior composition. The archive is not simply an abstract concrete sculpture: its form emerges from the relationship between distinct parts, with changes in height and direction giving the building a more complicated silhouette than a conventional office block.

The facade’s pronounced concrete ribs are also part of a broader architectural conversation. Konstantinovski studied at Yale shortly after Paul Rudolph’s Art and Architecture Building was completed. Scholarship on post-earthquake Skopje identifies the corrugated concrete surfaces of the Archive and the Goce Delčev Student Dormitory as evidence of this connection. The point is not that the Archive copies an American precedent, but that a language developed through international modernism was adapted to a new civic institution in Skopje.

The building rewards a comparison between drawing and built form. Start with the ground-floor plan: follow the edges of the separate components, then look up at the facade and consider how those divisions register externally. The elevation records more than a surface treatment; it shows how the building establishes its institutional presence through mass, depth, and rhythm.

Further readingMoMA’s collection records provide access to the original plan and elevation. Mirjana Lozanovska’s study, “Brutalism, Metabolism and its American Parallel,” places Konstantinovski’s work in a wider architectural context.

Goce Delčev Student Dormitory

Georgi Konstantinovski1969–77Student accommodation for approximately 1,200 residents

The dormitory’s most important architectural decision happens in plan. Rather than treating the building as a tower with rooms attached, Konstantinovski developed a repeatable residential unit and used its repetition to generate the larger structure.

An architectural study of the building describes the process as a geometric sequence: the student unit is translated to form a block, then the block is repeated with 90-degree rotations around a courtyard. The resulting composition gives the dormitory its irregular outline and varied heights. The bridges between blocks are therefore not decorative additions to an otherwise ordinary residence; they belong to a larger system of circulation and connection.

MoMA holds both the dormitory’s site plan and a floor plan, dated 1969–77. Read together, they reveal two scales of the same problem: how to organize individual rooms efficiently, and how to turn those repeated units into a large residential complex with shared spaces and routes between its parts. The building’s exterior makes that system visible through its towers, projecting balconies, bridges, and deeply textured concrete.

The facade also connects the project to the wider history of Brutalism. Lozanovska and the Toward a Concrete Utopia catalog relate Konstantinovski’s ribbed concrete surfaces to Paul Rudolph’s work at Yale. SOS Brutalism additionally notes that the grooves have been interpreted in relation to traditional Macedonian embroidery. These readings are not mutually exclusive, but they should be treated as interpretations of the surface rather than as a substitute for understanding the building’s underlying plan.

On siteLook for the bridges and changes in height, then return to the plan. The most useful question is how a repeated residential unit produces a form that feels irregular from the outside.

Further readingMoMA’s Goce Delčev Student Dormitory site and floor plans; Mirjana Lozanovska, “Brutalism, Metabolism and its American Parallel: Encounters in Skopje and in the Architecture of Georgi Konstantinovski.”

Museum of Contemporary Art (MSU)

Wacław Kłyszewski, Jerzy Mokrzyński, and Eugeniusz WierzbickiCompleted 1970Near the Kale Fortress

MSU’s three connected wings offer a different proposition from the monumental single-volume museum. The building distributes its program across linked parts, combining exhibition galleries with a cinema, library, archive, conservation spaces, and administrative facilities. Its design was donated by the Polish government after the 1963 earthquake and selected through a Polish architectural competition won by the team known as the “Polish Tigers.” The museum was completed in 1970.

The three-wing arrangement is worth considering as more than a formal gesture. A museum needs spaces with different requirements: galleries need flexibility for changing exhibitions; archives and conservation rooms require controlled conditions; a cinema has its own technical and acoustic demands. Connecting these functions without forcing them into one undifferentiated volume is the central organizational problem the plan addresses.

Its position also matters. The museum sits on a rise near the medieval fortress, with views over the city and a surrounding park that accommodates sculpture. Rather than being inserted into the street wall of the center, it occupies a distinct setting where the building, landscape, and collection can be experienced together. The museum itself identifies this relationship with the hill and surrounding panorama as a defining feature of the site.

The context is inseparable from the institution’s origins. MSU was established in 1964, following an international response to the earthquake and the donation of artworks from abroad. Its building was thus both a working museum and a public statement about Skopje’s cultural future. That significance comes from the conjunction of its program, its international origins, and its position in the reconstructed city—not from treating it as a symbol in isolation.

On siteWalk around the building before entering. Notice how the wings meet and how the museum’s profile changes against the hill. Inside, consider whether the different functions feel like parts of one institution or separate destinations.

Further readingThe Museum of Contemporary Art’s official building and history pages provide the clearest account of its program, origins, and setting.

Hydrometeorological Service Building

Krsto Todorovski1972–75Skupi Street

This is a more compact work than the large civic and commercial complexes elsewhere in the guide, but its documentation makes it worth the detour. The building’s architectural record includes an upper-floor plan, a facade section, and an axonometric drawing—three different ways of understanding how the design works.

The section is especially useful. A facade photograph shows the building’s outline; a section can explain how that outline relates to the interior, the floor levels, and the structural arrangement. The axonometric then helps connect the two-dimensional drawings to the building as a three-dimensional object. Together, these records offer a better basis for architectural analysis than assigning the building a broad label such as “Brutalist” and leaving it there.

Todorovski’s project is dated 1972–75 in the architectural record. Mirjana Lozanovska’s scholarship also discusses the National Hydraulic Institute, dated 1972, within the wider context of modern architecture in Skopje. The naming differs between sources, so the building is best identified by its current name, architect, and location rather than by assuming that every historical reference uses the same institutional title.

On siteCompare the facade with the published section and axonometric. Look for what the exterior conceals about the internal arrangement. This is the entry where the drawings matter most: they allow the building to be read as a designed system rather than simply photographed as a concrete object.

Further readingThe archival drawings and photographs published by Architectuul are a useful starting point. Its record includes the upper-floor plan, facade section, and axonometry.
