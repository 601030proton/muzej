Architecture | SkopjeSix buildings / A field guide

ConcreteSkopje

A field guide to the city’s modern architecture

Words Ace Trajkovski

Six buildings show how Skopje’s architects made room for public life after the earthquake: places to shop, study, work, keep records and encounter art.

After the earthquake of July 26, 1963, rebuilding Skopje meant restoring the institutions and everyday routines of a capital. Macedonian architects worked alongside colleagues from elsewhere in Yugoslavia and abroad. Their buildings gave that effort a lasting physical form.

This selection follows six different tasks, from student housing to a museum. Read them through their entrances, supports, passages and rooms. The notes below identify what to look for, link to drawings and research, and distinguish public routes from working or residential interiors.

01

Telecommunications Center
and Central Post Office

Infrastructure as a public room

Architect
Janko Konstantinov
Dates
Telecom: 1974 · Counter Hall: late 1970s–early 1980s
Location
Vardar riverfront, beside Goce Delčev Bridge

Sending a letter or making a telephone call once brought Skopje’s residents into an extraordinary civic interior. Konstantinov gave the postal counter hall a circular plan, with the public gathered beneath a roof carried by eight concrete columns. The columns incline into cantilevers and join a ring beam around the central cupola. The flowerlike silhouette follows this structural arrangement.

Beside it, the taller telecommunications building gives the complex a different scale. The two parts express different tasks: an institution’s technical and administrative work, and its encounter with the public. Their relationship is easier to see from the riverfront, where the vertical building and the spreading hall can be read together.

The 2013 fire destroyed the Counter Hall’s central cupola, skylights, interior fittings and murals by Macedonian painter Borko Lazeski. Much of the concrete structure survived. The distinction is important: the dramatic shell is only part of the work. Light, painted surfaces and the activity beneath the roof completed the room.

Europa Nostra and the European Investment Bank Institute’s 2023 report proposed restoring the damaged hall for cultural and social events. That would give its generous public space a purpose beyond the postal services for which it was designed.

Look forFrom the public riverfront, compare the tall telecommunications building with the radial Counter Hall. Look for the inclined concrete supports beneath the missing central roof.

AccessExterior study. Do not assume access to the fire-damaged Counter Hall.

02

City Trade Center
GTC / ГТЦ

A shopping center you can walk through

Architects
Živko Popovski and team
Dates
1969–73 · Competition: 1967
Location
Kej 13 Noemvri, central riverfront

GTC makes a simple offer to the city: you can pass through without buying anything. Its passages, stairs and terraces join the surrounding pedestrian routes. Shopping occupies those routes, but crossing the building is an everyday use in its own right.

The project also absorbed five residential towers already standing on the riverfront. Built before the earthquake, they became part of the new commercial complex. Reconstruction here meant working around surviving homes and bringing different generations of building into one arrangement.

Selected in the 1967 Yugoslav competition, the scheme was built from 1969 and opened in April 1973. Popovski’s collaborators included Tihomir Arsovski, Živko Gelevski, Dimitar Dimitrov, Blagoja Kolev, Radomir Lajović and Lidija Markova. Across five levels, the complex combined shops and services with separate arrangements for visitors and deliveries.

From within its passages, the distinction between building and street becomes less definite. Openings bring the city back into view; stairs offer another route across the same ground. GTC’s architectural achievement is this ease of passage. It gives the center places to stop as well as ways to keep moving.

Look forEnter from the city side and continue toward the riverfront. Watch how the existing residential towers rise out of the lower commercial levels.

AccessPublic shopping passages; individual businesses and upper areas have their own access arrangements.

03

City Archive
of Skopje

A building organized around what it protects

Architect
Georgi Konstantinovski
Dates
1966–68
Location
Moskovska 1, at Partizanski Odredi Boulevard

The Archive’s compact, largely closed upper mass gives little away. That closed appearance belongs to its purpose. Konstantinovski separated the entrance and supporting spaces from the silo-like volume holding the archive, giving storage a different architectural presence from the activities that serve it.

The composition is held to two axes of symmetry. Heavy load-bearing pillars mark the cut-off corners of its volumes; recessed openings interrupt the walls without dissolving their solidity. Read this arrangement before the surface texture. The building’s force comes from how its parts are held together.

Konstantinovski had studied at Yale with Paul Rudolph and Serge Chermayeff and worked in I. M. Pei’s office. The bush-hammered concrete recalls Rudolph’s Yale building. Here he put that roughness to a civic use: a small institution entrusted with the city’s records acquired the presence of a monument.

Its material character has proved vulnerable to ordinary repairs. SOS Brutalism’s February 2025 record documents replacement windows, altered roofing and new floor finishes introduced without conservation supervision. Preserving the outline alone does not preserve the depth, texture and proportions through which the building was composed.

Look forStart opposite the entrance. Trace the symmetry, the corner supports and the recesses beneath the storage mass. Compare the elevation with the ground-floor plan linked below.

AccessWorking archive. View from public space; arrange interior visits directly with the institution.

04

Goce Delčev
Student Dormitory

Rooms repeated into a collective home

Architect
Georgi Konstantinovski
Dates
1969–77 · Built in two phases
Location
Karpoš, west of the city center

Four blocks of different heights stand close enough to be joined by elevated bridges. The openings between them let the sky enter the composition. Individual rooms repeat across the facades, while the bridges make the larger institution visible: separate residential blocks form one connected complex.

The comparison with Konstantinovski’s Archive is revealing. There, symmetry gathers different functions into a compact figure. Here, repetition multiplies accommodation across several rising volumes. The textured concrete belongs to both projects, but the dormitory has to mediate between a student’s room and a much larger community.

Designed for approximately 1,200 residents, the complex was realized in two phases, from 1969–71 and 1973–77. SOS Brutalism connects its grooved concrete both to Konstantinovski’s American experience and to motifs from Macedonian embroidery. The latter is an interpretation worth considering in the patterned surface, rather than a claim that explains the whole building.

The dormitory also makes the limits of architectural admiration clear. Poor maintenance once left living conditions severely degraded. Renovations completed in stages through 2020 addressed rooms, bathrooms, infrastructure and shared facilities. A successful conservation account must include the people sleeping and studying behind the concrete.

Look forFrom public space, find a view through a gap between blocks. Notice how the bridges connect them without filling the opening.

AccessStudent residence. Observe from public approaches and request permission before entering residential areas.

05

Museum of
Contemporary Art

A permanent home for solidarity

Architects
Wacław Kłyszewski, Jerzy Mokrzyński & Eugeniusz Wierzbicki
Dates
Opened 1970 · Institution founded 1964
Location
Samoilova 17, Kale hill

MSU began with a collection before it had a purpose-built home. Following the 1963 earthquake, artists donated works to Skopje; the city established the museum in February 1964. Its early exhibitions used other galleries and rented premises. The opening of this building in 1970 allowed the collection to be presented together in its first permanent exhibition.

The Polish government donated the architectural design, selected through a national competition. Kłyszewski, Mokrzyński and Wierzbicki arranged the museum in three connected wings. Exhibition rooms sit within a larger institution that includes a cinema, library, archives, conservation facilities and storage. Solidarity became something the city could maintain, study and show.

The site changes the approach. On Kale hill, beside the medieval fortress, the museum stands clear of the center’s continuous street frontage. Its low, spreading form leaves the surrounding landscape and city panorama in view. The park provides space for sculpture, extending the experience of the collection beyond the galleries.

Its place in this guide is deliberate. The quieter museum broadens a selection otherwise dominated by heavy concrete forms. Skopje’s reconstruction produced different architectural answers, including a building whose public role depends on the relationship between art, rooms and open ground.

Look forApproach through the grounds and compare the low wings with the city below. Inside, follow the exhibition route rather than expecting every wing to be publicly accessible.

AccessTicketed museum. Check the museum’s website for opening hours, exhibitions and visitor information.

06

Hydrometeorological
Service Building

A different scale inside

Architect
Krsto Todorovski
Dates
1972–75
Location
Skupi Street

Todorovski’s building presents a dense assembly of projecting volumes and cylindrical supports. From outside, it can look forbiddingly heavy. Architects Marija Mano Velevska and Slobodan Velevski describe a different interior: smaller pockets of working space combined with shared rooms, giving the institution an unexpectedly domestic scale.

That observation gives the archival drawings a purpose. Read the upper-floor plan alongside the facade section: the exterior’s separate projections belong to an arrangement of smaller spaces. In the published axonometric, their relationship becomes easier to follow around the building. Its outline is the result of that assembly.

Velevska and Velevski also record the interior’s use for the 2021 Biennale of Young Artists. A scientific workplace could accommodate a cultural event because its internal spaces offered more than corridors between offices. This is a specific example of an institutional building finding another public use.

The architectural record identifies the project as the Hydrometeorological Service Building, also known as RHMZ. That is the building discussed here. Its value lies in the contrast between a monumental exterior and the more intimate spaces described within it.

Look forStudy the projecting volumes and cylindrical supports from the public approach. Open the archived plan and section below to compare their external shape with the subdivision inside.

AccessInstitutional workplace. Exterior viewing is the basis of this entry; arrange any interior visit in advance.

What the city keeps

The six buildings carry different ideas of public life. GTC lets a walk become part of a shopping center. The Archive gives records a protected home. At Goce Delčev, a room belongs to a larger residential community; at MSU, donated artworks became a collection with a permanent address.

Their future depends on decisions as concrete as their construction: keeping a passage open, repairing a roof, making a student room habitable, finding a use for a damaged hall. These are ways of caring for the city that Skopje’s architects built.
